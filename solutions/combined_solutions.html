Paper 1 — BNK 221: Corporate Financing Decision

Tribhuvan University · Faculty of Management · BBA 8th Semester · Model Question 2026 · Full Marks 100 · Time 3 Hrs

Group A — Brief Answer Questions (10 × 2 = 20)

Q1. What do you mean by corporate financing decision?

Solution:

Corporate financing decision is the decision about how a firm raises the funds needed for its investments — i.e. the choice of sources, amount, mix and timing of long-term and short-term funds so that the cost of capital is minimised and the value of the firm is maximised.

Key points: - Capital structure decision: proportion of debt, preferred stock and equity (financing mix). - Choice of instruments: shares, debentures, term loans, leases, convertibles, warrants, short-term credit, etc. - Maturity mix: balance of long-term and short-term financing (matching principle). - Timing and method of issue: when and how to raise funds (public issue, rights, private placement), considering market conditions, risk, control and flexibility.

Q2. Define preemptive right.

Solution:

Preemptive right is the right of existing common shareholders to purchase any new issue of common stock (or securities convertible into it) in proportion to their existing holding before it is offered to outsiders.

Key points: - It protects shareholders against dilution of control (voting power). - It protects against dilution of value, since new shares cannot be sold to outsiders at a low price to the detriment of old shareholders. - It is exercised through a rights offering; each old share receives one right. - Rights can be exercised, sold in the market, or allowed to lapse.

Q3. List out the major types leases.

Solution:

A lease is a contract in which the owner of an asset (lessor) gives the right to use the asset to another party (lessee) in return for periodic rental payments. Major types:

  1. Operating (service) lease – short-term, cancellable, lessor bears maintenance and obsolescence risk.
  2. Financial (capital) lease – long-term, non-cancellable, fully amortized; lessee bears maintenance and risk.
  3. Sale-and-leaseback – firm sells its own asset to a lessor and immediately leases it back.
  4. Leveraged lease – a three-party lease in which the lessor finances a large part of the asset cost with borrowed funds from a lender.
  5. Direct lease – lessee acquires the use of an asset it did not own before, directly from manufacturer/leasing company.

Q4. Write the meaning of trust-receipt loan.

Solution:

A trust-receipt loan is a form of secured short-term inventory financing in which the borrower holds the financed goods in trust for the lender. The lender (bank/finance company) pays for the goods; the borrower signs a trust receipt acknowledging that the goods are held in trust and that the sale proceeds will be remitted to the lender immediately when the goods are sold.

Key points: - Used for high-value, identifiable items (automobiles, appliances, equipment) — often called floor planning. - Each item is identified by serial number in the trust receipt. - Title (security interest) remains with the lender until the loan is repaid.

Q5. Define financial distress.

Solution:

Financial distress is a situation in which a firm's operating cash flows are not sufficient to meet its current obligations (such as interest and principal payments), forcing it to take corrective action.

Key points: - It ranges from technical insolvency (unable to pay bills as they fall due) to legal bankruptcy (liabilities exceed assets). - It is mainly caused by excessive use of debt (high financial leverage) together with business risk. - Costs of financial distress: direct costs (legal, administrative costs of bankruptcy) and indirect costs (lost sales, loss of key employees, stricter supplier terms, underinvestment). - These costs limit the amount of debt in the trade-off theory of capital structure.

Q6. What is meant by call feature of bond?

Solution:

A call feature (call provision) gives the issuing company the right to redeem (buy back) the bond before maturity at a pre-specified price called the call price, usually par value plus a call premium.

Key points: - Firms call bonds mainly when interest rates fall, to refund at a lower coupon (bond refunding). - It also allows removal of restrictive covenants and forcing conversion of convertibles. - It is disadvantageous to investors, so callable bonds carry a higher coupon. - There is often a call-protection (deferred call) period during which the bond cannot be called.

Q7. Write about founders and angels in financing.

Solution:

Founders and angels are the earliest sources of equity for a start-up business.

  • Founders' financing: the entrepreneur(s) invest their own savings, along with funds from family and friends ("bootstrapping"). It shows commitment and keeps full control, but the amount is limited.
  • Angel investors (business angels): wealthy individuals who invest their personal money in early-stage (seed) ventures in exchange for equity or convertible debt.
  • Angels usually invest before venture capital, take high risk, and often provide advice, mentoring and network contacts besides money.
  • They expect high returns through a later exit (VC round, IPO or sale of the firm).

Q8. What do you mean by moratorium period in a term loan?

Solution:

Moratorium period (grace period) in a term loan is the initial period after disbursement during which the borrower is not required to repay the principal (and sometimes not even the interest).

Key points: - It is granted because a new project needs time (construction, trial run, gestation) before it generates cash flows. - Interest during the moratorium may be paid, capitalized, or deferred as per the agreement. - After the moratorium, the loan is repaid in instalments (EMI/equal principal) over the remaining term. - It reduces the early cash-flow burden and improves the project's repayment capacity.

Q9. Star Traders buys under terms of 3/10 net 50. Star Trader does not take discount and pays on 60th day. What is the annual percentage cost of stretching accounts payable? Assume 360 days in a year.

Solution:

Given: - Credit terms = 3/10, net 50 → discount (d) = 3%, discount period = 10 days, net period = 50 days - Actual payment day (stretched) = 60th day - Days in a year = 360

Formula:

Annual cost of not taking discount = (d ÷ (100 − d)) × (360 ÷ (Days paid − Discount period))

Step-by-step calculation:

  1. Cost per period = 3 ÷ (100 − 3) = 3 ÷ 97 = 0.030928 = 3.0928%
  2. Extra days of credit obtained = 60 − 10 = 50 days
  3. Number of such periods in a year = 360 ÷ 50 = 7.2
  4. Annual percentage cost = 3.0928% × 7.2 = 22.27%

(For comparison, if paid on the 50th day: (3/97) × (360/40) = 27.84%. Stretching lowers the cost.)

Answer / Decision: The annual percentage cost of stretching accounts payable to 60 days is 22.27%.

💡 Exam Hint (remember): Cost = (d/(100−d)) × (360/(Payment day − Discount days)). Trap: use the actual payment day (60), not the net period (50), and divide by (100 − d), not 100. Shortcut: 3/97 ≈ 3.09%; × 360/50 = 7.2 → ≈ 22.27%.

Q10. Shikhar Company has 500,000 shares outstanding and the firm's charter provides for a cumulative voting procedure. The company has nine directors. What is the minimum number of shares needed to ensure the election of one director?

Solution:

Given: - Total shares outstanding (S) = 500,000 - Total directors to be elected (N) = 9 - Desired directors (r) = 1

Formula:

Shares required = (r × S) ÷ (N + 1) + 1

Step-by-step calculation:

  1. r × S = 1 × 500,000 = 500,000
  2. N + 1 = 9 + 1 = 10
  3. 500,000 ÷ 10 = 50,000
  4. Shares required = 50,000 + 1 = 50,001 shares

Answer / Decision: A minimum of 50,001 shares is needed to ensure the election of one director.

💡 Exam Hint (remember): Shares needed = (r × S)/(N + 1) + 1. Trap: forgetting the "+1" in the denominator (N+1) or the final "+1" share. Shortcut: for one director, simply S/(N+1) + 1.

Q11. Describe the features of optimal capital structure.

Solution:

Optimal capital structure is the mix of debt, preferred stock and common equity that minimises the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and maximises the market value of the firm and its share price. A sound (optimal) capital structure should have the following features:

  1. Profitability (Return): It should make maximum use of financial leverage at minimum cost so that EPS and return on equity are maximised. Cheaper debt should be used as long as return on assets exceeds the cost of debt.

  2. Solvency / Conservatism (Risk): Debt should not be used to the extent that it threatens the firm's solvency. The firm's cash flows must be able to cover fixed charges (interest, principal, preferred dividends) comfortably — measured by interest coverage and debt-service coverage ratios.

  3. Flexibility: The structure should allow the firm to raise additional funds or reduce funds when needed, e.g. by keeping unused borrowing capacity, using callable bonds, and avoiding restrictive covenants.

  4. Control: It should minimise dilution of control of existing shareholders. Excessive issue of equity dilutes control, whereas debt does not carry voting rights.

  5. Minimum cost of capital / Maximum value: The mix should result in the lowest overall cost of capital and highest firm value.

  6. Simplicity and capacity: The structure should be simple to manage and within the debt capacity of the firm; it should not involve too many types of securities.

  7. Matching and suitability: It should suit the nature of the business, stability of earnings, asset structure (tangible assets support more debt) and market conditions.

Conclusion: An optimal capital structure balances return and risk — using enough debt to gain tax and leverage benefits but not so much that financial distress costs offset them.

Q12. Discuss the merits and demerits of long-term debt as a source of financing.

Solution:

Long-term debt refers to borrowing with maturity of more than one year — debentures/bonds and term loans — carrying a fixed interest obligation and repayment of principal.

Merits (from the firm's/shareholders' viewpoint): 1. Lower cost: Debt is less risky for investors, so the required return is lower than equity. 2. Tax advantage: Interest is tax-deductible, so the after-tax cost = kd(1 − T) is further reduced; it creates an interest tax shield. 3. No dilution of control: Debt holders have no voting rights, so ownership control remains with existing shareholders. 4. Financial leverage: If return on investment exceeds the cost of debt, EPS and ROE increase (trading on equity). 5. Fixed obligation: Debt holders do not share in excess profits; in inflation, repayment is made in cheaper money. 6. Flexibility: Callable debt can be retired when not needed or when rates fall.

Demerits: 1. Fixed charge burden: Interest and principal must be paid regardless of earnings; failure leads to default. 2. Increased financial risk: Higher debt raises the variability of EPS and the risk of financial distress and bankruptcy, increasing the cost of equity. 3. Restrictive covenants: Indentures may restrict dividends, additional borrowing, sale of assets, and working capital. 4. Maturity/refinancing risk: Principal must be repaid on maturity, possibly in unfavourable market conditions. 5. Collateral requirement: Secured debt ties up assets and reduces future borrowing capacity. 6. Limited capacity: A firm can borrow only up to a certain limit; beyond it, cost rises sharply.

Conclusion: Long-term debt is beneficial for firms with stable earnings and tangible assets, but it must be used within prudent limits.

Q13. Explain the concept and importance of venture capital financing.

Solution:

Concept: Venture capital (VC) is equity or equity-linked long-term financing provided by professional investors (venture capital firms/funds) to new, young, high-risk and high-growth potential businesses, usually unlisted, that cannot obtain funds from conventional sources. The VC takes an ownership stake, participates actively in management (board seat, advice) and exits after 5–7 years through an IPO, trade sale or buy-back, earning a high capital gain.

Stages of VC financing: seed/start-up, first stage (early), second stage (expansion), mezzanine/bridge financing, and buyout financing.

Importance of venture capital: 1. Financing for start-ups: Provides funds to innovative firms that lack track record and collateral and cannot get bank loans or access the stock market. 2. Promotes innovation and technology: Supports new products, technology and research-based ventures. 3. Value-added services: VCs offer managerial expertise, strategic guidance, contacts, and help in recruitment and marketing. 4. Risk sharing: As equity holders, VCs share the business risk; no fixed repayment burden on the young firm. 5. Entrepreneurship and employment: Encourages entrepreneurs, creates employment and contributes to economic growth. 6. Path to capital market: Prepares firms for an IPO, developing the capital market. 7. High return for investors: Gives investors an opportunity for high returns through portfolio diversification across ventures.

Nepalese context: Private equity and venture capital funds (PEVC) are now registered and regulated under SEBON's Specialized Investment Fund Regulation, supporting start-ups and SMEs.

Q14. What is leveraged buyout? Describe the characteristics of successful LBO.

Solution:

Leveraged Buyout (LBO): An LBO is the acquisition of a company (or a division) by a small group of investors — often including existing management — financed mostly with borrowed funds (often 70–90% debt), using the target firm's own assets and future cash flows as security. After the buyout, the firm usually becomes privately held (going private). The debt is repaid from operating cash flows and sale of non-core assets; investors earn profit by later re-listing (reverse LBO) or selling the firm. When managers buy the firm it is called a Management Buyout (MBO).

Characteristics of a successful LBO candidate: 1. Stable and predictable cash flows: Needed to service heavy interest and principal payments. 2. Low existing debt: A firm with little debt has unused borrowing capacity to support the LBO debt. 3. Separable, non-core assets: Assets/divisions that can be sold quickly to repay part of the debt. 4. Competent and committed management: Experienced managers, often with an ownership stake, who are motivated to improve efficiency. 5. Low capital expenditure requirements: Cash is needed for debt service, not heavy reinvestment. 6. Strong market position: An established brand or market share in a mature industry with limited competition. 7. Not high-tech / not subject to rapid change: Mature, low-growth businesses with low technological and business risk are better candidates. 8. Tangible assets for collateral and potential for cost reduction or efficiency gains. 9. Purchase at a reasonable price: Overpaying ruins the return.

Conclusion: LBOs create value through tax savings on interest, improved management incentives and efficiency, but high leverage makes them risky if cash flows decline.

Q15. Differentiate between operating lease and financial lease.

Solution:

An operating lease is a short-term, cancellable lease in which the lessor retains the risks and rewards of ownership, while a financial lease is a long-term, non-cancellable lease that transfers substantially all risks and rewards of ownership to the lessee.

Q16. The Mega Company has warrants outstanding that expire in three years. Each warrant entitles the holder to purchase two shares of common stock at an exercise price of Rs 150 per share. Determine theoretical value and premium over the theoretical value if the respective prices of common stock and warrants are:

a. Rs 140 per share and Rs 10 per warrant. b. Rs 160 per share and Rs 40 per warrant.

Solution:

Given: - Shares per warrant (N) = 2 - Exercise price (E) = Rs 150 per share - (a) Ps = Rs 140, warrant price = Rs 10 - (b) Ps = Rs 160, warrant price = Rs 40

Formula: - Theoretical value of warrant (TV) = max[(Ps − E) × N, 0] - Premium over theoretical value = Market price of warrant − TV - Premium per share = Premium ÷ N; % premium = Premium ÷ TV × 100

Step-by-step calculation:

(a) Ps = Rs 140, warrant = Rs 10 1. (Ps − E) × N = (140 − 150) × 2 = −10 × 2 = −20 → negative, so TV = Rs 0 (a warrant cannot have negative value; holder simply won't exercise). 2. Premium = 10 − 0 = Rs 10 per warrant. 3. Premium per share = 10 ÷ 2 = Rs 5. 4. % premium over TV is not defined (TV = 0); the entire market price is premium (time value/speculative value).

(b) Ps = Rs 160, warrant = Rs 40 1. TV = (160 − 150) × 2 = 10 × 2 = Rs 20 2. Premium = 40 − 20 = Rs 20 per warrant 3. Premium per share = 20 ÷ 2 = Rs 10 4. % premium over TV = 20 ÷ 20 × 100 = 100%

Case Ps (Rs) TV (Rs) Market price of warrant (Rs) Premium (Rs) Premium per share (Rs)
a 140 0 10 10 5
b 160 20 40 20 10

Answer / Decision: (a) TV = Rs 0, premium = Rs 10; (b) TV = Rs 20, premium = Rs 20. The warrant sells above its theoretical value because of its leverage and the chance that the share price will rise before expiry (3 years remaining).

💡 Exam Hint (remember): TV = (Ps − E) × N, never below zero. Trap: forgetting to multiply by the number of shares per warrant (N = 2) or writing a negative TV. Shortcut: Premium = Warrant price − TV; if Ps < E, the whole warrant price is premium.

Q17. Akash Food Company (AFC) projects an increase in sales from Rs 1,200,000 to Rs 1,800,000 but it needs an additional Rs 200,000 of current assets to support this expansion. The money can be obtained from the bank at an interest rate of 12 percent, discount interest; no compensating balance is required. Alternatively, AFC can finance the expansion by no longer taking discounts, thus increasing accounts payable. AFC purchases on the terms of 3/10, net 40, but it can delay payment for an additional 20 days and thus becoming 20 days past due without a penalty.

Based strictly on effective annual interest rate comparisons, how should AFC finance its expansion?

Solution:

Given: - Funds needed = Rs 200,000 - Bank loan: 12% discount interest, no compensating balance - Trade credit: 3/10, net 40; payment can be stretched 20 days → paid on day 60 - Year = 360 days

Formula: - Discount loan: Effective rate = Interest rate ÷ (1 − Interest rate) - Trade credit: Cost = (d ÷ (100 − d)) × (360 ÷ (Payment day − Discount period))

Step-by-step calculation:

1. Bank loan (discount interest) - Effective rate = 0.12 ÷ (1 − 0.12) = 0.12 ÷ 0.88 = 0.13636 = 13.64% - (Check: to get Rs 200,000 usable, borrow 200,000 ÷ 0.88 = Rs 227,273; interest = 227,273 × 12% = Rs 27,273; 27,273 ÷ 200,000 = 13.64%.)

2. Trade credit (forgo discount, pay on day 60) - Payment day = 40 + 20 = 60 - Cost = (3 ÷ 97) × (360 ÷ (60 − 10)) = 0.030928 × 7.2 = 0.2227 = 22.27% - Without stretching (paid on day 40): (3 ÷ 97) × (360 ÷ 30) = 0.030928 × 12 = 37.11%

Source Effective annual cost
Bank loan (12% discount interest) 13.64%
Trade credit, paid on day 60 (stretched) 22.27%
Trade credit, paid on day 40 (not stretched) 37.11%

Answer / Decision: The bank loan costs 13.64%, which is lower than the 22.27% cost of trade credit even after stretching. AFC should finance the expansion with the bank loan and continue taking the cash discounts.

💡 Exam Hint (remember): Discount loan rate = r/(1 − r); trade credit cost = (d/(100−d)) × 360/(pay day − discount days). Trap: using the net period (40) instead of the stretched day (60), or forgetting that discount interest raises the effective rate above 12%. Shortcut: choose the source with the lower effective rate — here 13.64% < 22.27%, so borrow.

Q18. Explain the methods of issuing securities. Also discuss the process of initial public offering.

Solution:

A. Methods of issuing securities

Firms raise long-term funds from the primary market through the following methods:

  1. Public issue (general cash offer): Securities are offered to the general public through a prospectus. - Initial Public Offering (IPO): the first issue of shares to the public by a company, after which it becomes listed. - Further Public Offering (FPO) / seasoned offering: an additional issue by an already-listed company.

  2. Rights issue (privileged subscription): New shares are offered first to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, usually at a price below market, honouring the preemptive right. It is cheap and preserves control. In Nepal, banks and other listed companies widely use rights issues (e.g. 1:1, 10:3).

  3. Private placement: Securities are sold directly to a small number of institutional investors (banks, insurance companies, pension funds, mutual funds) without a public offer. It is quick, flexible and cheaper, but the issue size is limited and investors may demand higher return.

  4. Underwritten (firm-commitment / bought deal) offering: The underwriter (investment bank/merchant banker) buys the whole issue at a fixed price and resells it to the public, bearing the risk of unsold shares. In a bought deal, a single underwriter buys the entire issue quickly.

  5. Best-efforts offering: The investment banker acts only as an agent and sells as much as possible; unsold securities are returned to the issuer. Risk remains with the issuer.

  6. Competitive bidding vs. negotiated offering: In competitive bidding, the issuer invites bids from several underwriters and awards to the highest bidder; in a negotiated offer, the terms are negotiated with one underwriter.

  7. Book building: The price is discovered through bids from investors within a price band; the final price is fixed based on demand. SEBON introduced book building (for qualified institutional investors) in Nepal.

  8. Shelf registration: A firm registers a large issue once and sells portions over a period (up to two years) as needed.

  9. Other methods: Employee share issue, preferential allotment, and offers for sale by existing promoters.

B. Process of Initial Public Offering (Nepalese context)

  1. Decision to go public: Board and general meeting approve the IPO, amend the memorandum/articles and convert to a public limited company if needed.
  2. Appointing an issue manager: A SEBON-licensed merchant banker (issue manager) is appointed to manage the issue; underwriters are appointed to guarantee subscription.
  3. Due diligence and preparation: The issue manager examines financial statements, legal documents, projections and business plans.
  4. Credit rating: IPO must be graded by a credit rating agency (e.g. ICRA Nepal, CARE Ratings Nepal).
  5. Preparing the prospectus and SEBON approval: The draft prospectus with company details, objectives, risk factors and financials is submitted to the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON), which reviews and approves it.
  6. Pricing the issue: Fixed-price at par (Rs 100) for most companies, or at a premium (approved by SEBON) / via book building for eligible firms.
  7. Marketing and announcement: Publication of the issue opening notice; road shows/promotion; collection centres (ASBA – member banks) and online application through MeroShare (CDS & Clearing Ltd.).
  8. Subscription period: Investors apply through ASBA within the open period; quotas for employees, mutual funds, project-affected locals and Nepali workers abroad are reserved.
  9. Allotment: If oversubscribed, shares are allotted by lottery/pro-rata under SEBON rules; refunds are released.
  10. Dematerialisation and listing: Allotted shares are credited to investors' demat accounts at CDS and Clearing Ltd., and the shares are listed on NEPSE for trading in the secondary market.
  11. Post-issue compliance: Reporting to SEBON/NEPSE and continuing disclosure obligations.

Conclusion: Choosing the proper method depends on the issue size, cost, urgency, control considerations and market conditions; an IPO converts a private firm into a listed public company, giving it access to a wider capital base and liquidity for its shares.

Q19. The Nepal Energy Company has announced a rights offer to raise Rs 600 million for a new Gas Station. The stock currently sells for Rs 600 per share, and there are 4.8 million shares outstanding. Par value is Rs 100.

a. What is the maximum possible subscription price? What is the minimum? b. If the subscription price is set at Rs 500 per share, how many shares must be sold? c. How many rights will it take to buy one share? d. What is the value of a right? e. What is the ex-rights price? f. Show how a shareholder with 1,000 shares before the offering and no desire (or money) to buy additional shares is not harmed by the rights offer.

Solution:

Given: - Funds to be raised = Rs 600,000,000 - Current market price (Po, rights-on) = Rs 600 - Shares outstanding (No) = 4,800,000 - Par value = Rs 100; Subscription price (S) = Rs 500

Formula: - New shares = Funds required ÷ Subscription price - Rights per new share (N) = Old shares ÷ New shares - Value of right (rights-on) R = (Po − S) ÷ (N + 1) - Ex-rights price Px = (N × Po + S) ÷ (N + 1); value of right (ex-rights) R = (Px − S) ÷ N

Step-by-step calculation:

(a) Maximum and minimum subscription price - Maximum = Rs 600 (the current market price). If the subscription price were above Rs 600, nobody would subscribe, since shares can be bought more cheaply in the market. (In practice it is set slightly below market to ensure success.) - Minimum = Rs 100 (par value). Under the Companies Act, shares cannot be issued at a discount below par value. Any price between Rs 100 and Rs 600 is possible.

(b) Number of new shares - New shares = 600,000,000 ÷ 500 = 1,200,000 shares

(c) Rights needed to buy one share - N = 4,800,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 4 rights per new share (ratio 1 new share for every 4 held)

(d) Value of a right - Rights-on: R = (600 − 500) ÷ (4 + 1) = 100 ÷ 5 = Rs 20 - Check (ex-rights): R = (580 − 500) ÷ 4 = 80 ÷ 4 = Rs 20

(e) Ex-rights price - Px = (4 × 600 + 500) ÷ (4 + 1) = (2,400 + 500) ÷ 5 = 2,900 ÷ 5 = Rs 580 - Or Px = Po − R = 600 − 20 = Rs 580 - (Check by total value: (4,800,000 × 600 + 600,000,000) ÷ 6,000,000 = 3,480,000,000 ÷ 6,000,000 = Rs 580)

(f) Position of a shareholder with 1,000 shares

Position Option 1: Sell rights Option 2: Exercise rights Option 3: Do nothing
Wealth before offer 1,000 × 600 = 600,000 1,000 × 600 = 600,000 600,000
Shares after offer 1,000 1,000 + 1,000/4 = 1,250 1,000
Value of shares after 1,000 × 580 = 580,000 1,250 × 580 = 725,000 580,000
Cash from sale of rights 1,000 × 20 = 20,000 — 0
Cash paid for new shares — 250 × 500 = (125,000) —
Net wealth after 600,000 725,000 − 125,000 = 600,000 580,000 (loss 20,000)

Answer / Decision: (a) Max Rs 600, Min Rs 100; (b) 1,200,000 shares; (c) 4 rights; (d) Rs 20; (e) Rs 580; (f) A shareholder who does not want to buy simply sells the 1,000 rights for Rs 20,000; share value falls to Rs 580,000 but total wealth remains Rs 600,000 — no loss. Only a shareholder who neither exercises nor sells the rights (lets them lapse) loses Rs 20,000.

💡 Exam Hint (remember): R = (Po − S)/(N + 1) rights-on; Px = (N·Po + S)/(N + 1); R = (Px − S)/N ex-rights. Trap: using N instead of N + 1 with the rights-on price, or forgetting that the minimum price is par value. Shortcut: N = old shares ÷ new shares; Px = Po − R.

Q20. The Kathmandu Electronic Company (KEC) intends to borrow Rs 800,000 to support its short-term financing requirements during the next year. The financing alternatives offered by the bank include:

Alternative 1: A discount interest loan with a simple interest of 15 percent and no compensating balance requirement. Alternative 2: A 14 percent simple interest loan that has a 15 percent compensating balance requirement. Alternative 3: Rs 1 million revolving line of credit with simple interest of 12 percent paid on the amount borrowed and a 1 percent commitment fee. a. Compute the effective cost (rate) of each financing alternative assuming KEC borrows Rs 800,000. Which alternative should it use? b. Discuss the factors other than cost to be considered while choosing a bank for short-term loan.

Solution:

(a) Effective cost of each alternative

Given: - Usable funds needed = Rs 800,000; period = 1 year - Alt 1: 15% discount interest - Alt 2: 14% simple interest, 15% compensating balance (CB) - Alt 3: Rs 1,000,000 revolving credit, 12% on amount used, 1% commitment fee on unused portion

Formula: - Discount loan: Effective rate = r ÷ (1 − r) - Simple interest with CB: Effective rate = r ÷ (1 − CB) - Revolving credit: Effective rate = (Interest + Commitment fee) ÷ Usable funds - Amount to borrow for required usable funds = Usable funds ÷ (1 − r or CB)

Step-by-step calculation:

Alternative 1 – Discount interest loan (15%) - Effective rate = 0.15 ÷ (1 − 0.15) = 0.15 ÷ 0.85 = 17.65% - To have Rs 800,000 usable: borrow = 800,000 ÷ 0.85 = Rs 941,176 - Interest = 941,176 × 15% = Rs 141,176 - Check: 141,176 ÷ 800,000 = 17.65% - (If face value borrowed is only Rs 800,000: interest 120,000; usable 680,000; 120,000 ÷ 680,000 = 17.65% — same rate.)

Q1. Define risk, peril and hazard.

Solution:

  • Risk: Uncertainty about the occurrence of a loss. In other words, it is the chance that the actual outcome will differ from the expected outcome (objective risk) or a person's doubt about the outcome (subjective risk).
  • Peril: The cause of loss. Examples: fire, flood, earthquake, theft, accident, death.
  • Hazard: A condition that increases the frequency or severity of loss. It has four types:
  • Physical hazard: a physical condition such as icy roads, faulty wiring or a defective lock.
  • Moral hazard: dishonesty or bad character that increases loss, e.g. faking an accident or inflating a claim.
  • Attitudinal (morale) hazard: carelessness or indifference to loss because insurance exists, e.g. leaving the car unlocked.
  • Legal hazard: features of the legal system or regulatory environment that increase loss, e.g. very large court awards.

Q2. What do you mean by systemic risk?

Solution:

Systemic risk is the risk that the failure of one institution or market segment spreads through interconnections and causes the collapse or serious disruption of the entire financial system or economy. Examples are the 2008 global financial crisis (failure of Lehman Brothers) and a bank run that spreads to many banks.

Key points: - It arises from interconnectedness and contagion ("domino effect"). It is not caused by an event that hits everyone at once. - It is different from systematic risk, which is economy-wide, non-diversifiable market risk such as inflation, interest-rate or recession risk. - It cannot be handled by private insurance because losses are catastrophic and correlated. It is managed through regulation, capital and solvency requirements, central bank or lender-of-last-resort support, and supervision. In Nepal this is done by Nepal Rastra Bank and the Nepal Insurance Authority.

Q3. List out the objectives of risk management.

Solution:

Risk management has objectives before a loss happens and after it happens.

Pre-loss objectives: 1. Economy: prepare for potential losses in the most economical way (cost of safety programmes, premiums, etc.). 2. Reduction of anxiety: reduce worry and fear about possible losses. 3. Meeting legal (externally imposed) obligations: e.g. safety rules, compulsory third-party motor insurance, Social Security Fund contributions.

Post-loss objectives: 1. Survival of the firm. 2. Continued operation of the business. 3. Stability of earnings. 4. Continued growth. 5. Social responsibility: minimise the effect of the loss on employees, customers, suppliers and the community.

Q4. Define risk pooling.

Solution:

Risk pooling (pooling of losses) means spreading the losses of a few people over the whole group. Each member pays a small certain amount (the premium) in place of a large uncertain loss. In this way, the average loss replaces the actual loss.

Key points: - It is the essence of insurance. - It is based on the law of large numbers. The larger the pool of independent exposures, the more accurately actual losses can be predicted, and the lower the relative risk (variation) for each member. - It requires many similar, independent (uncorrelated) exposure units. - Example: if 1,000 houses each pay Rs 2,000 into a pool, the Rs 20,00,000 collected can pay for the few houses that actually burn.

Q5. Define insurance and list down its basic characteristics.

Solution:

Insurance is the pooling of fortuitous losses by transferring such risks to an insurer. In return for a premium, the insurer agrees to indemnify insureds for such losses, provide other pecuniary benefits when a loss occurs, or render services connected with the risk (Rejda).

Basic characteristics: 1. Pooling of losses: the losses of the few are spread over the many, and average loss replaces actual loss. 2. Payment of fortuitous losses: only losses that are unforeseen, unexpected and accidental are paid. Intentional losses are not paid. 3. Risk transfer: pure risk is transferred from the insured to an insurer who is financially stronger. 4. Indemnification: the insured is restored, partly or fully, to the financial position held before the loss.

Q7. Define loss forecasting. List out the common techniques of loss forecasting.

Solution:

Loss forecasting means predicting the likely frequency and severity of future losses from past loss data and relevant factors. Its purpose is to choose suitable risk-control and risk-financing techniques, set retention levels and budget for losses.

Common techniques: 1. Probability analysis: assigns probabilities to events based on past data, e.g. the chance of fire in a year. 2. Forecasting based on loss distributions: fits past losses to a statistical distribution, such as the normal distribution, to estimate the expected loss and the range of loss. 4. Q12. Explain the concept and significance of law of large number in risk pooling?

Solution:

Concept

The law of large numbers says that as the number of exposure units (the sample) increases, the actual results get closer to the expected results. Put another way, the relative variation of actual losses from expected losses becomes smaller.

For example, if the probability of a house fire is 1%, an insurer with 10 houses cannot predict losses. An insurer with 100,000 similar houses can expect about 1,000 fires and will be fairly accurate.

Conditions for it to work - There must be a large number of homogeneous, similar exposure units. - Losses must be independent (not correlated or catastrophic). - The loss must be accidental and measurable.

Significance in risk pooling 1. Accurate prediction of losses: the insurer can estimate the expected loss and charge a fair premium. 2. Reduction of objective risk: risk falls in proportion to 1/√N. As the pool grows, uncertainty for the insurer falls. 3. Basis for premium calculation: the expected loss per unit becomes the pure premium, and loading is added to it. 4. Makes insurance affordable: each member pays a small, certain premium in place of a large, uncertain loss. 5. Financial stability of insurers: predictable claims allow proper reserves and solvency. 6. Explains why insurers avoid catastrophic risks: correlated losses break the law. Examples are earthquake losses in one area, as in Nepal in 2015. Such risks need reinsurance, for example with Nepal Re-Insurance Company.

Conclusion: The law of large numbers is the mathematical foundation of insurance. It turns individual uncertainty into group predictability.

Q13. Define adverse selection. Explain the tools used by insurance companies to control the chance of adverse selection.

Solution:

Adverse selection is the tendency of people with a higher-than-average chance of loss to seek insurance at standard (average) rates. If this is not controlled, actual losses become higher than expected. It arises from asymmetric information: the applicant knows more about their own risk than the insurer does. Examples are a person with heart disease buying life or health insurance, or a house owner in a flood zone buying flood cover.

Tools to control adverse selection

  1. Careful underwriting: the insurer selects and classifies applicants. It may accept them at standard rates, accept them at higher rates, or reject them.
  2. Medical examination and proposal form: medical tests, health questionnaires and the duty of utmost good faith reveal hidden risk factors.
  3. Risk classification and rating: applicants are grouped by age, health, occupation and location. Higher-risk groups pay higher premiums, for example substandard rating with extra premium.
  4. Policy provisions: - Pre-existing condition exclusion and waiting period in health insurance. - Suicide clause: suicide within the first 1–2 years is not covered. - Incontestable or contestable period: the insurer can contest the policy for misrepresentation within 2 years. - Exclusions for war, hazardous activities and similar risks.
  5. Deductibles and coinsurance: the insured shares part of the loss. This discourages high-risk or careless buyers.
  6. Information sharing: insurers share claim data, the motor claim history and blacklists through the regulator or industry databases.
  7. Group underwriting: people are insured as a group (for example, employees) who joined for reasons other than buying insurance. This dilutes individual adverse selection.

Q14. Explain about premature death. Also explain its financial implications.

Solution:

Premature death is the death of a family head with unfulfilled financial obligations. These include dependants to support, children to educate, and a mortgage or other debts to pay off. A death is "premature" because of these financial responsibilities, not only because of age.

Financial implications (costs of premature death) 1. Loss of human life value: the family permanently loses its share of the deceased's future earnings. This loss can be very large. 2. Additional expenses: funeral expenses, medical bills of the last illness, estate settlement costs and unpaid debts. 3. Insufficient income for the family: many families do not have enough savings or insurance. Their living standard falls, and children's education may stop. 4. Non-economic costs: emotional grief, loss of a role model, loss of guidance for children, and loss of household services.

When premature death causes financial insecurity - When the deceased has dependants (spouse, children, parents). - When there are outstanding debts such as a home loan or car loan. - When there are children to educate. - When a single-parent family loses its only earner. - When there is insufficient life insurance or savings.

A person with no dependants, for example a single person, causes little financial hardship to others.

Q18. Explain the concept and process of risk management.

Solution:

Concept of risk management

Risk management is a systematic process for identifying and evaluating pure loss exposures faced by an organisation or individual, and for selecting and implementing the most appropriate techniques for treating those exposures (Rejda). Its aim is to protect assets, earnings and people at the lowest possible cost. The broader Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) approach covers pure, speculative, strategic, financial and operational risks together.

It has pre-loss objectives (economy, reduction of anxiety, meeting legal obligations) and post-loss objectives (survival, continued operation, stable earnings, continued growth and social responsibility).

Process of risk management

Step 1: Identify loss exposures

All potential losses are identified: - Property exposures: buildings, equipment, inventory, vehicles. - Liability exposures: defective products, environmental pollution, professional errors. - Business income exposures: loss of profit due to interruption. - Human resource exposures: death, disability or retirement of key employees, and work injury. - Crime, cyber, and foreign/political exposures.

Sources of information include questionnaires, physical inspection, flowcharts, financial statements, past loss data and the RMIS.

Step 2: Measure and analyse the loss exposures

Each exposure is estimated for: - Loss frequency: the probable number of losses. - Loss severity: the probable size of losses, including the maximum possible loss and the probable maximum loss.

Exposures are then ranked by importance. Severity is more important than frequency because a single catastrophic loss can destroy a firm.

Step 3: Select the appropriate combination of techniques

A. Risk control (reduce frequency or severity): - Avoidance: not acquiring or abandoning the exposure, e.g. not producing a risky product. - Loss prevention: reducing frequency, e.g. safety training, maintenance. - Loss reduction: reducing severity, e.g. sprinklers, fire alarms, rehabilitation. - Duplication: keeping back-ups or spares, e.g. data backups. - Separation: dividing assets among several locations. - Diversification: spreading business across products, customers or markets.

B. Risk financing (pay for losses that occur): - Retention: the firm pays losses itself, through current income, funded reserves, a credit line or a captive insurer. It is suitable for high-frequency, low-severity losses. - Non-insurance transfers: contracts, leases, hold-harmless agreements, hedging, incorporation. - Commercial insurance: suitable for low-frequency, high-severity losses. It involves choosing the insurer, coverage, deductibles and limits.

Risk management matrix:

Low severity High severity
Low frequency Retention Insurance
High frequency Loss prevention + retention Avoidance / loss reduction

Step 4: Implement and monitor the risk management programme - Prepare a risk management policy statement. - Coordinate with other departments (accounting, finance, HR, production). - Monitor and review the programme periodically, because exposures, laws and costs change. Use risk management audits and the RMIS.

Conclusion: Risk management is a continuous cycle: identify, measure, treat and monitor. It lets an organisation lower the cost of risk, maintain stability and achieve its goals.

Q19. Explain the fundamental legal principles of insurance contract.

Solution:

An insurance contract is a legal contract. It must meet general contract requirements and also follow certain special principles.

A. Fundamental legal principles

1. Principle of indemnity

The insurer should restore the insured to approximately the same financial position held before the loss. The insured should not profit. This prevents overinsurance and reduces moral hazard. Loss is measured by: - Actual cash value (ACV) = replacement cost − depreciation (or fair market value, or the broad evidence rule). - Replacement cost insurance: no deduction for depreciation. - Valued policy: a fixed amount is paid for total loss, e.g. antiques or paintings.

Exceptions: valued policies, valued policy laws, replacement cost insurance, and life insurance (a valued contract, because human life cannot be measured).

2. Principle of insurable interest

The insured must stand to lose financially if the loss occurs. Its purposes are to prevent gambling, reduce moral hazard and measure the loss. In property insurance it must exist at the time of loss. In life insurance it must exist only at the inception of the policy. Self, spouse, dependants, creditors and business partners have insurable interest.

3. Principle of subrogation

After paying the loss, the insurer takes over the insured's legal rights to recover from a negligent third party. Its purposes are to prevent the insured from collecting twice, hold the guilty party responsible, and keep premiums low. It does not apply to life insurance or most personal health insurance.

4. Principle of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei)

Both parties must be completely honest. This principle is supported by three doctrines: - Representations: statements made by the applicant. A material, false representation that is relied upon makes the contract voidable. - Concealment: intentional failure to disclose a material fact makes the contract voidable. - Warranty: a statement or promise that is part of the contract, such as "a watchman will be on duty". A breach allows the insurer to deny the claim.

5. Principle of proximate cause

The insurer is liable only if the dominant, effective cause (causa proxima) of the loss is an insured peril. Example: fire caused by lightning is covered, while fire caused by war, if war is excluded, is not.

6. Principle of contribution

When the same property is insured with more than one insurer, the insurers share the loss in proportion to their coverage (pro-rata liability). The insured cannot recover more than the actual loss. This is a corollary of indemnity.

7. Principle of loss minimisation

The insured must take reasonable steps to minimise the loss, acting as an uninsured prudent person would.

B. Requirements of a valid insurance contract 1. Offer and acceptance: usually, the proposal by the applicant is the offer, and the issue of the policy by the insurer is the acceptance. 2. Consideration: the premium and the promise to follow policy conditions. 3. Competent parties: the parties must be of legal age and sound mind, and the insurer must be licensed (by the NIA in Nepal). 4. Legal purpose: contracts that encourage illegal acts are void.

C. Distinct legal characteristics

Characteristic Meaning
Aleatory Values exchanged are unequal and depend on chance
Unilateral Only the insurer makes a legally enforceable promise
Conditional The insurer must pay only if the insured meets the policy conditions
Personal Property insurance insures the person, not the property; assignment needs consent
Contract of adhesion The insurer drafts the terms; ambiguities are interpreted in favour of the insured

Conclusion: These principles keep insurance fair, prevent gambling and fraud, and preserve its basic purpose of indemnity.

Q20. Explain about agriculture insurance. Why do you think agriculture insurance is important for countries like Nepal?

Solution:

Concept of agriculture insurance

Agriculture insurance protects farmers against financial losses to crops, livestock, poultry and fishery caused by natural perils, diseases and other uncontrollable events. It compensates them so they can continue farming.

Types of agriculture insurance 1. Crop insurance: covers paddy, maize, wheat, vegetables, fruits, tea, coffee, etc. against drought, flood, hailstorm, landslide, pest and disease. 2. Livestock insurance: covers cattle, buffalo, goats, pigs, etc. against death due to disease or accident. 3. Poultry insurance: covers birds against death due to disease (e.g. bird flu) or accident. 4. Fishery insurance: covers fish ponds against disease, flood and pollution. 5. Others: bee-keeping, greenhouse/tunnel farming and plantation insurance.

Approaches - Indemnity-based insurance: compensation is based on the actual assessed loss of the farm. It is accurate but has high assessment costs and moral hazard. - Index-based (parametric) insurance: payment is triggered by an index such as rainfall, temperature or area yield. There is no farm-level loss assessment, settlement is fast and costs are low, but there is basis risk (the farm loss may not match the index).

Agriculture insurance in Nepal - It started under the Agriculture Insurance Directive (2069 B.S.) issued by Beema Samiti, now the Nepal Insurance Authority (NIA). - The government provides a premium subsidy of approximately 80% (as per the directive). The farmer pays only about 20%. - Non-life insurers are mandated to provide agriculture and livestock insurance. Reinsurance support comes from Nepal Re-Insurance Company.

Importance of agriculture insurance for Nepal 1. Dependence on agriculture: over 60% of the population depends on agriculture for a living, and it contributes about a quarter of GDP. Protecting farmers protects the economy. 2. High exposure to climate and natural risks: monsoon floods, landslides, drought, hailstorms, cold waves and climate change cause frequent and severe crop and livestock losses. 3. Income stability and poverty reduction: compensation prevents farmers from falling into debt traps and distress sales of assets. 4. Access to credit: insured crops and livestock act as collateral. Banks and cooperatives lend more readily, which supports agricultural financing. 5. Encourages commercialisation and modern technology: farmers can take the risk of investing in high-value crops, improved breeds and commercial farms. 6. Food security: quick recovery after losses maintains production and national food supply. 7. Reduces the government's disaster relief burden: a planned risk transfer replaces ad hoc relief. 8. Reduces migration: more stable farm income reduces youth migration abroad. 9. Rural development and financial inclusion: brings rural farmers into the formal financial system.

Challenges in Nepal: low awareness, small and fragmented landholdings, difficult loss assessment, moral hazard and fraud, delays in claim settlement, limited data for index insurance, and low insurer interest because of high costs.

Way forward: awareness programmes, index-based products, digital claim processes, timely release of subsidy and cooperative-based distribution.

Conclusion: For an agrarian, disaster-prone country like Nepal, agriculture insurance is a vital tool for protecting farmers' livelihoods, ensuring food security and promoting agricultural growth.

Q21. Insurance is one of the heavily regulated area of the overall financial system. In light of this statement, explain the reasons for insurance regulations.

Solution:

Introduction

Insurance is regulated heavily because insurers collect premiums in advance and promise to pay large sums far into the future. Contracts are complex, and the public depends on insurers to remain solvent. Failure of an insurer harms policyholders and the economy. In Nepal, insurance is regulated by the Nepal Insurance Authority (NIA), formerly Beema Samiti, under the Insurance Act, 2079.

Reasons for insurance regulation

1. Maintain insurer solvency

This is the most important reason. Premiums are paid in advance, but claims may be paid many years later, especially in life insurance. Regulation ensures that insurers have enough capital, reserves and solvency margins, and sound investments, so they can pay claims. Insolvency would cause great hardship to policyholders.

2. Compensate for inadequate consumer knowledge

Insurance contracts are technical and complex. Most consumers cannot judge policy terms, prices or the financial strength of insurers. Regulators approve policy forms and provide information to protect consumers.

3. Ensure reasonable rates

Rates should be adequate (to keep insurers solvent), not excessive (to avoid overcharging) and not unfairly discriminatory. In Nepal, some rates are tariff-based, e.g. motor third-party and agriculture.

4. Make insurance available

Regulation ensures availability of essential coverages to all, including high-risk or underserved segments. Examples are compulsory third-party motor cover, subsidised agriculture and livestock insurance, micro-insurance, and the requirement to open branches in rural areas.

5. Prevent unfair practices and protect policyholders

Regulation prevents misleading advertising, twisting, rebating, misrepresentation by agents and unfair claim practices. It also handles consumer complaints and ensures fair and prompt claim settlement.

6. Insurance is affected with a public interest

Insurance supports credit, trade and families' financial security, and it mobilises long-term savings for investment. Its failure has wide social and economic consequences, and it is a source of potential systemic risk.

Methods of regulation - Legislation: acts passed by parliament, e.g. the Insurance Act, 2079. - Courts: decisions interpreting contracts and the constitutionality of laws. - Regulatory authority: a government agency, the NIA, issues regulations, directives and circulars, licenses insurers and agents, and supervises them.

Conclusion: Insurance is heavily regulated because it is a long-term promise of future payment and is affected with a strong public interest. Regulation protects policyholders, keeps insurers solvent and maintains public confidence in the financial system.

Q1. Why is a derivative instrument known as a zero-sum game?

Solution:

A derivative is called a zero-sum game because whatever one party gains, the counterparty loses by exactly the same amount (ignoring transaction costs).

  • Every derivative contract has two sides — a long (buyer) and a short (seller/writer). It is a side-bet on the price of the underlying asset; no new wealth is created.
  • Example: if a futures price rises by Rs 10, the long gains Rs 10 and the short loses Rs 10; the sum of gains and losses is zero.
  • In options, the premium received by the writer equals the premium paid by the buyer, and the buyer's payoff at expiry is the writer's loss.
  • After commissions and taxes it becomes slightly negative-sum for traders, but the derivative itself only transfers risk and wealth between parties.

Q2. Why do investors pay more for an American option than similar European option?

Solution:

Investors pay more for an American option because it gives everything a European option gives plus the right of early exercise at any time up to expiry.

  • A European option can be exercised only on the expiration date; an American option can be exercised on or before that date.
  • Extra rights never reduce value, so American option price ≥ European option price (same S, X, T, σ, r).
  • Early exercise can be valuable — e.g., a deep in-the-money American put (receive X now and earn interest on it) or an American call just before a large dividend.
  • The difference (early-exercise premium) is the price of this flexibility; for a call on a non-dividend-paying stock it is zero, so both are equal.

Q3. An investor who wants to achieve maximum gain and accept a higher maximum loss selects a call option with a lower exercise price. Justify.

Solution:

The statement is correct: a lower-strike call costs more (higher maximum loss) but pays more at every expiry price (higher gain).

  • A call with a lower exercise price is more in-the-money, so its premium is higher. Since the maximum loss of a long call = premium paid, the maximum loss is higher.
  • Profit at expiry = max(0, S_T − X) − C. For any S_T above the strikes, a lower X gives a larger payoff (S_T − X), and the extra payoff exceeds the extra premium when the price rises strongly.
  • The lower-strike call also starts paying off at a lower stock price, so the chance of gain is higher.
  • Hence an aggressive investor who wants the larger possible gain and can bear a larger premium loss selects the lower exercise price (e.g., X = 100, C = 12 vs X = 110, C = 6: at S_T = 140 profit is 28 vs 24; max loss is 12 vs 6).

Q4. A longer-lived option has a higher price. Do you agree? Give reasons.

Solution:

Yes, generally a longer-lived option is worth more because it has more time value.

  • More time means a greater chance that the stock price moves favourably (higher probability of ending deep in-the-money), while the loss is still limited to the premium.
  • For American options (calls and puts) it is always true: a longer option has all the exercise opportunities of the shorter one plus more, so it can never be worth less.
  • For European calls on non-dividend stocks it is also true, as longer time lowers the PV of the exercise price.
  • Exception: a European put (deep in-the-money, where receiving X later lowers its PV) or a European call on a stock paying a large dividend before the longer expiry may be worth less.

Q5. Define future contract.

Solution:

A futures contract is a standardised, exchange-traded agreement between two parties to buy or sell a specified quantity of an asset at a price fixed today (the futures price) for delivery on a specified future date.

  • Standardised: quantity, quality, delivery date and place are fixed by the exchange.
  • Clearinghouse: it becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, eliminating default risk.
  • Margin and marking-to-market: initial and maintenance margins are required and gains/losses are settled daily.
  • Liquidity/offsetting: most positions are closed by an opposite trade before delivery. Unlike a forward, it is not customised or private.

Q6. What do you mean by upside capture in portfolio insurance?

Solution:

Upside capture is the percentage of the uninsured portfolio's value (or gain) that the insured portfolio retains when the market rises.

  • Upside capture = Value of insured portfolio ÷ Value of uninsured portfolio (in a rising market) × 100.
  • In portfolio insurance with protective puts, part of the funds is spent on put premiums, so fewer shares are held. When prices rise, the puts expire worthless and the insured portfolio is worth less than the uninsured one.
  • Therefore upside capture is always less than 100%; the shortfall is the cost of insurance.
  • Example: insured value Rs 92,300 vs uninsured Rs 100,000 → upside capture = 92.3%.

Q7. Under what conditions is a long hedge using futures suitable?

Solution:

A long hedge (buying futures) is suitable when a firm plans to buy an asset in the future and fears that its price will rise (i.e., it is effectively short in the spot market).

  • Example: a flour mill that must buy wheat in three months, an airline that must buy jet fuel, or an importer who must pay in dollars later.
  • Buying futures today locks in the purchase price: if the spot price rises, the gain on futures offsets the higher cost of buying the asset.
  • It is also used by someone who has short-sold an asset and must buy it back later (anticipatory hedge).
  • Suitable when futures on the same or a highly correlated asset are available, so basis risk is small.

Q8. Write the meaning of synthetic instruments.

Solution:

Synthetic instruments are combinations of other securities/derivatives that replicate the payoff of a given instrument exactly, so they should have the same price (law of one price).

  • They are based mainly on put–call parity: S + P = C + X/(1 + r)^T.
  • Synthetic call = long stock + long put − borrow PV(X); synthetic put = long call − long stock + lend PV(X).
  • Synthetic stock = long call − long put + lend PV(X); synthetic forward/futures = long call + short put (same strike).
  • Uses: to create a position when the actual one is unavailable or expensive, and to exploit arbitrage when actual and synthetic prices differ.

Q9. Why do investors prefer bull spreads from call options over those from put options?

Solution:

Both spreads profit from a moderate price rise, but a call bull spread (buy low-strike call, sell high-strike call) is often preferred because:

  • Net debit, known risk: the investor pays a net premium upfront; the maximum loss equals this premium and is known in advance. A put bull spread (buy low-strike put, sell high-strike put) produces a net credit but requires the writer to post margin.
  • Less early-assignment risk: the short high-strike put in a put spread is in-the-money when prices fall and can be exercised early (American puts); the short out-of-the-money call in a call spread is rarely exercised early.
  • Simpler and intuitive: buying calls to gain from a rise is easier to understand and has lower margin/cash-management problems.
  • The put version is chosen only when it offers a better net premium.

Q10. If you want to change the fixed-rate liability in to foliating-rate liability using a swap, how would you do it?

Solution:

Enter a plain-vanilla interest rate swap as the fixed-rate receiver and floating-rate payer (a pay-floating, receive-fixed swap).

  • The firm continues to pay the fixed coupon on its existing loan/bond.
  • Under the swap, it receives a fixed rate (which offsets the fixed coupon) and pays a floating rate (e.g., SOFR + spread).
  • Net cost = Fixed paid on loan − Fixed received in swap + Floating paid ≈ Floating rate (± spread).
  • Example: loan at 10% fixed; swap: receive 9.5% fixed, pay SOFR → net cost = SOFR + 0.5%, i.e., a floating-rate liability. Useful when rates are expected to fall.

Q11. Describe the role of the derivative market.

Solution:

Derivative markets (options, forwards, futures and swaps) play an important role in modern financial systems. Their main roles are:

  1. Risk management (hedging): The most important role. Derivatives allow firms and investors to transfer unwanted price risk to those willing to bear it. Exporters hedge currency risk with forwards, farmers hedge crop prices with futures, banks hedge interest rate risk with swaps, and investors insure portfolios with puts.

  2. Price discovery: Futures and options prices reflect the market's collective expectations of future spot prices and volatility. Because trading is cheap and fast, new information is reflected first in derivative prices, which helps producers and consumers plan production and inventory.

  3. Operational advantages: Derivatives have lower transaction costs than the spot market, higher liquidity, and make short selling easy (sell a futures or buy a put instead of borrowing shares). Large positions can be taken or reversed quickly.

  4. Market efficiency: Arbitrage between spot and derivative markets (e.g., cash-and-carry, put–call parity) keeps prices of related assets in line, so mispricing is quickly eliminated and markets become more efficient.

  5. Speculation and leverage: Speculators can take positions with a small initial outlay (margin or premium). They add liquidity and are the counterparties who absorb hedgers' risk.

  6. Completing the market: Derivatives create payoff patterns (e.g., limited loss, insured portfolios, synthetic positions) that cannot be obtained easily with stocks and bonds alone, widening investors' choices.

Criticism/caution: Misuse (excessive speculation, poor understanding) can create large losses (e.g., Barings, 2008 crisis), so proper regulation and risk management are required. In Nepal, a formal derivative exchange is still developing, but forward contracts in foreign exchange and commodity markets are used.

Q21. Explain the margin rule and the margin operation process by a clearinghouse in option trading.

Solution:

Meaning of margin in options

Margin is a performance bond (good-faith deposit) that a trader must keep with the broker/clearinghouse to ensure that contractual obligations will be met. In options, the right belongs to the buyer and the obligation to the writer, so margin rules differ for buyers and writers.

Margin rules

  1. Option buyers: The buyer has a right but no obligation. Therefore, the buyer pays the full premium upfront (no buying on margin for short-term options; long-term options may be partly margined). The maximum loss is the premium, so no further margin is needed.

  2. Covered call writers: A writer who owns the underlying stock (covered call) needs no additional margin; the shares are deposited/held with the broker and can be delivered if exercised.

  3. Uncovered (naked) call writers: Because the potential loss is unlimited, the writer must deposit: - Margin = 100% of the premium received + 20% of the underlying stock value − amount by which the option is out-of-the-money, - subject to a minimum of premium + 10% of the stock value. - Example: S = 100, X = 105, premium = 4 for 100 shares: 400 + 0.20 × 10,000 − 500 = 1,900; minimum = 400 + 1,000 = 1,400 → margin = Rs 1,900.

  4. Uncovered put writers: Margin = premium + 20% of stock value − out-of-the-money amount, minimum premium + 10% of the exercise price. A cash-secured put (cash equal to the strike deposited) needs no additional margin.

  5. Spreads and combinations: For spreads, the margin is based on the maximum possible loss (difference between strikes minus net premium), since the long leg protects the short leg.

  6. Margins are recomputed daily as prices change; if the margin is insufficient, a margin call is issued.

Margin operation process by the clearinghouse

  1. Novation / guarantee: The clearinghouse (e.g., OCC in the USA) becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. It guarantees the performance of every contract, eliminating counterparty default risk.
  2. Clearing members: Only clearing members deal directly with the clearinghouse. Brokers who are not members clear through members. Customers post margin with brokers, brokers with clearing members, and clearing members with the clearinghouse.
  3. Daily collection and marking-to-market: The clearinghouse values all short positions daily and collects additional margin from clearing members whose positions have lost value; members in turn collect from customers.
  4. Margin calls and liquidation: If a member or customer fails to meet a call, the position is closed out and the margin used to cover losses.
  5. Exercise and assignment: When a buyer exercises, the clearinghouse assigns the exercise randomly to a clearing member with a short position, who then assigns it to one of its customer writers (random or FIFO).
  6. Clearing/guarantee fund and default waterfall: If a member defaults, losses are met first from the defaulter's margin, then its clearing fund contribution, then the clearinghouse's own capital and the contributions of other members.

Nepalese context: NEPSE does not yet have an organised options exchange; SEBON has issued commodity/derivative regulations and the market is at an early stage, so these margin practices are drawn from international exchanges.

Conclusion: Margin rules protect the system from writers' default, while the clearinghouse's daily margining, guarantee and assignment process makes option markets safe and liquid.
