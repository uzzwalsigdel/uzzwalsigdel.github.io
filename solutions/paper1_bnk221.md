## Group A — Brief Answer Questions (10 × 2 = 20)

### Q1. What do you mean by corporate financing decision?

**Solution:**

**Corporate financing decision** is the decision about *how* a firm raises the funds needed for its investments — i.e. the choice of sources, amount, mix and timing of long-term and short-term funds so that the cost of capital is minimised and the value of the firm is maximised.

Key points:
- **Capital structure decision:** proportion of debt, preferred stock and equity (financing mix).
- **Choice of instruments:** shares, debentures, term loans, leases, convertibles, warrants, short-term credit, etc.
- **Maturity mix:** balance of long-term and short-term financing (matching principle).
- **Timing and method of issue:** when and how to raise funds (public issue, rights, private placement), considering market conditions, risk, control and flexibility.

### Q2. Define preemptive right.

**Solution:**

**Preemptive right** is the right of existing common shareholders to purchase any new issue of common stock (or securities convertible into it) **in proportion to their existing holding before it is offered to outsiders**.

Key points:
- It protects shareholders against **dilution of control** (voting power).
- It protects against **dilution of value**, since new shares cannot be sold to outsiders at a low price to the detriment of old shareholders.
- It is exercised through a **rights offering**; each old share receives one right.
- Rights can be exercised, sold in the market, or allowed to lapse.

### Q3. List out the major types leases.

**Solution:**

A **lease** is a contract in which the owner of an asset (lessor) gives the right to use the asset to another party (lessee) in return for periodic rental payments. Major types:

1. **Operating (service) lease** – short-term, cancellable, lessor bears maintenance and obsolescence risk.
2. **Financial (capital) lease** – long-term, non-cancellable, fully amortized; lessee bears maintenance and risk.
3. **Sale-and-leaseback** – firm sells its own asset to a lessor and immediately leases it back.
4. **Leveraged lease** – a three-party lease in which the lessor finances a large part of the asset cost with borrowed funds from a lender.
5. **Direct lease** – lessee acquires the use of an asset it did not own before, directly from manufacturer/leasing company.

### Q4. Write the meaning of trust-receipt loan.

**Solution:**

A **trust-receipt loan** is a form of **secured short-term inventory financing** in which the borrower holds the financed goods **in trust for the lender**. The lender (bank/finance company) pays for the goods; the borrower signs a trust receipt acknowledging that the goods are held in trust and that the **sale proceeds will be remitted to the lender** immediately when the goods are sold.

Key points:
- Used for high-value, identifiable items (automobiles, appliances, equipment) — often called **floor planning**.
- Each item is identified by serial number in the trust receipt.
- Title (security interest) remains with the lender until the loan is repaid.

### Q5. Define financial distress.

**Solution:**

**Financial distress** is a situation in which a firm's operating cash flows are **not sufficient to meet its current obligations** (such as interest and principal payments), forcing it to take corrective action.

Key points:
- It ranges from **technical insolvency** (unable to pay bills as they fall due) to **legal bankruptcy** (liabilities exceed assets).
- It is mainly caused by excessive use of debt (high financial leverage) together with business risk.
- **Costs of financial distress:** direct costs (legal, administrative costs of bankruptcy) and indirect costs (lost sales, loss of key employees, stricter supplier terms, underinvestment).
- These costs limit the amount of debt in the trade-off theory of capital structure.

### Q6. What is meant by call feature of bond?

**Solution:**

A **call feature (call provision)** gives the **issuing company the right to redeem (buy back) the bond before maturity** at a pre-specified price called the **call price**, usually par value plus a **call premium**.

Key points:
- Firms call bonds mainly when **interest rates fall**, to refund at a lower coupon (bond refunding).
- It also allows removal of restrictive covenants and forcing conversion of convertibles.
- It is disadvantageous to investors, so callable bonds carry a **higher coupon**.
- There is often a **call-protection (deferred call) period** during which the bond cannot be called.

### Q7. Write about founders and angels in financing.

**Solution:**

**Founders and angels** are the earliest sources of equity for a start-up business.

- **Founders' financing:** the entrepreneur(s) invest their own savings, along with funds from family and friends ("bootstrapping"). It shows commitment and keeps full control, but the amount is limited.
- **Angel investors (business angels):** wealthy individuals who invest their personal money in early-stage (seed) ventures in exchange for equity or convertible debt.
- Angels usually invest **before venture capital**, take high risk, and often provide **advice, mentoring and network contacts** besides money.
- They expect high returns through a later exit (VC round, IPO or sale of the firm).

### Q8. What do you mean by moratorium period in a term loan?

**Solution:**

**Moratorium period (grace period)** in a term loan is the **initial period after disbursement during which the borrower is not required to repay the principal** (and sometimes not even the interest).

Key points:
- It is granted because a new project needs time (construction, trial run, gestation) before it generates cash flows.
- Interest during the moratorium may be paid, capitalized, or deferred as per the agreement.
- After the moratorium, the loan is repaid in instalments (EMI/equal principal) over the remaining term.
- It reduces the early cash-flow burden and improves the project's repayment capacity.

### Q9. Star Traders buys under terms of 3/10 net 50. Star Trader does not take discount and pays on 60th day. What is the annual percentage cost of stretching accounts payable? Assume 360 days in a year.

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Credit terms = 3/10, net 50 → discount (d) = 3%, discount period = 10 days, net period = 50 days
- Actual payment day (stretched) = 60th day
- Days in a year = 360

**Formula:**

Annual cost of not taking discount = (d ÷ (100 − d)) × (360 ÷ (Days paid − Discount period))

**Step-by-step calculation:**

1. Cost per period = 3 ÷ (100 − 3) = 3 ÷ 97 = 0.030928 = 3.0928%
2. Extra days of credit obtained = 60 − 10 = 50 days
3. Number of such periods in a year = 360 ÷ 50 = 7.2
4. Annual percentage cost = 3.0928% × 7.2 = **22.27%**

(For comparison, if paid on the 50th day: (3/97) × (360/40) = 27.84%. Stretching lowers the cost.)

**Answer / Decision:** The annual percentage cost of stretching accounts payable to 60 days is **22.27%**.

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** Cost = (d/(100−d)) × (360/(Payment day − Discount days)). Trap: use the *actual* payment day (60), not the net period (50), and divide by (100 − d), not 100. Shortcut: 3/97 ≈ 3.09%; × 360/50 = 7.2 → ≈ 22.27%.

### Q10. Shikhar Company has 500,000 shares outstanding and the firm's charter provides for a cumulative voting procedure. The company has nine directors. What is the minimum number of shares needed to ensure the election of one director?

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Total shares outstanding (S) = 500,000
- Total directors to be elected (N) = 9
- Desired directors (r) = 1

**Formula:**

Shares required = (r × S) ÷ (N + 1) + 1

**Step-by-step calculation:**

1. r × S = 1 × 500,000 = 500,000
2. N + 1 = 9 + 1 = 10
3. 500,000 ÷ 10 = 50,000
4. Shares required = 50,000 + 1 = **50,001 shares**

**Answer / Decision:** A minimum of **50,001 shares** is needed to ensure the election of one director.

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** Shares needed = (r × S)/(N + 1) + 1. Trap: forgetting the "+1" in the denominator (N+1) or the final "+1" share. Shortcut: for one director, simply S/(N+1) + 1.

## Group B — Descriptive Answer Questions (Any six, 6 × 5 = 30)

### Q11. Describe the features of optimal capital structure.

**Solution:**

**Optimal capital structure** is the mix of debt, preferred stock and common equity that **minimises the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) and maximises the market value of the firm and its share price**. A sound (optimal) capital structure should have the following features:

1. **Profitability (Return):** It should make maximum use of financial leverage at minimum cost so that EPS and return on equity are maximised. Cheaper debt should be used as long as return on assets exceeds the cost of debt.

2. **Solvency / Conservatism (Risk):** Debt should not be used to the extent that it threatens the firm's solvency. The firm's cash flows must be able to cover fixed charges (interest, principal, preferred dividends) comfortably — measured by interest coverage and debt-service coverage ratios.

3. **Flexibility:** The structure should allow the firm to raise additional funds or reduce funds when needed, e.g. by keeping unused borrowing capacity, using callable bonds, and avoiding restrictive covenants.

4. **Control:** It should minimise dilution of control of existing shareholders. Excessive issue of equity dilutes control, whereas debt does not carry voting rights.

5. **Minimum cost of capital / Maximum value:** The mix should result in the lowest overall cost of capital and highest firm value.

6. **Simplicity and capacity:** The structure should be simple to manage and within the debt capacity of the firm; it should not involve too many types of securities.

7. **Matching and suitability:** It should suit the nature of the business, stability of earnings, asset structure (tangible assets support more debt) and market conditions.

**Conclusion:** An optimal capital structure balances return and risk — using enough debt to gain tax and leverage benefits but not so much that financial distress costs offset them.

### Q12. Discuss the merits and demerits of long-term debt as a source of financing.

**Solution:**

**Long-term debt** refers to borrowing with maturity of more than one year — debentures/bonds and term loans — carrying a fixed interest obligation and repayment of principal.

**Merits (from the firm's/shareholders' viewpoint):**
1. **Lower cost:** Debt is less risky for investors, so the required return is lower than equity.
2. **Tax advantage:** Interest is tax-deductible, so the after-tax cost = kd(1 − T) is further reduced; it creates an interest tax shield.
3. **No dilution of control:** Debt holders have no voting rights, so ownership control remains with existing shareholders.
4. **Financial leverage:** If return on investment exceeds the cost of debt, EPS and ROE increase (trading on equity).
5. **Fixed obligation:** Debt holders do not share in excess profits; in inflation, repayment is made in cheaper money.
6. **Flexibility:** Callable debt can be retired when not needed or when rates fall.

**Demerits:**
1. **Fixed charge burden:** Interest and principal must be paid regardless of earnings; failure leads to default.
2. **Increased financial risk:** Higher debt raises the variability of EPS and the risk of financial distress and bankruptcy, increasing the cost of equity.
3. **Restrictive covenants:** Indentures may restrict dividends, additional borrowing, sale of assets, and working capital.
4. **Maturity/refinancing risk:** Principal must be repaid on maturity, possibly in unfavourable market conditions.
5. **Collateral requirement:** Secured debt ties up assets and reduces future borrowing capacity.
6. **Limited capacity:** A firm can borrow only up to a certain limit; beyond it, cost rises sharply.

**Conclusion:** Long-term debt is beneficial for firms with stable earnings and tangible assets, but it must be used within prudent limits.

### Q13. Explain the concept and importance of venture capital financing.

**Solution:**

**Concept:** **Venture capital (VC)** is equity or equity-linked long-term financing provided by professional investors (venture capital firms/funds) to **new, young, high-risk and high-growth potential businesses**, usually unlisted, that cannot obtain funds from conventional sources. The VC takes an ownership stake, participates actively in management (board seat, advice) and exits after 5–7 years through an IPO, trade sale or buy-back, earning a high capital gain.

**Stages of VC financing:** seed/start-up, first stage (early), second stage (expansion), mezzanine/bridge financing, and buyout financing.

**Importance of venture capital:**
1. **Financing for start-ups:** Provides funds to innovative firms that lack track record and collateral and cannot get bank loans or access the stock market.
2. **Promotes innovation and technology:** Supports new products, technology and research-based ventures.
3. **Value-added services:** VCs offer managerial expertise, strategic guidance, contacts, and help in recruitment and marketing.
4. **Risk sharing:** As equity holders, VCs share the business risk; no fixed repayment burden on the young firm.
5. **Entrepreneurship and employment:** Encourages entrepreneurs, creates employment and contributes to economic growth.
6. **Path to capital market:** Prepares firms for an IPO, developing the capital market.
7. **High return for investors:** Gives investors an opportunity for high returns through portfolio diversification across ventures.

**Nepalese context:** Private equity and venture capital funds (PEVC) are now registered and regulated under SEBON's Specialized Investment Fund Regulation, supporting start-ups and SMEs.

### Q14. What is leveraged buyout? Describe the characteristics of successful LBO.

**Solution:**

**Leveraged Buyout (LBO):** An LBO is the **acquisition of a company (or a division) by a small group of investors — often including existing management — financed mostly with borrowed funds** (often 70–90% debt), using the target firm's own assets and future cash flows as security. After the buyout, the firm usually becomes **privately held** (going private). The debt is repaid from operating cash flows and sale of non-core assets; investors earn profit by later re-listing (reverse LBO) or selling the firm. When managers buy the firm it is called a **Management Buyout (MBO)**.

**Characteristics of a successful LBO candidate:**
1. **Stable and predictable cash flows:** Needed to service heavy interest and principal payments.
2. **Low existing debt:** A firm with little debt has unused borrowing capacity to support the LBO debt.
3. **Separable, non-core assets:** Assets/divisions that can be sold quickly to repay part of the debt.
4. **Competent and committed management:** Experienced managers, often with an ownership stake, who are motivated to improve efficiency.
5. **Low capital expenditure requirements:** Cash is needed for debt service, not heavy reinvestment.
6. **Strong market position:** An established brand or market share in a mature industry with limited competition.
7. **Not high-tech / not subject to rapid change:** Mature, low-growth businesses with low technological and business risk are better candidates.
8. **Tangible assets for collateral** and potential for cost reduction or efficiency gains.
9. **Purchase at a reasonable price:** Overpaying ruins the return.

**Conclusion:** LBOs create value through tax savings on interest, improved management incentives and efficiency, but high leverage makes them risky if cash flows decline.

### Q15. Differentiate between operating lease and financial lease.

**Solution:**

An **operating lease** is a short-term, cancellable lease in which the lessor retains the risks and rewards of ownership, while a **financial lease** is a long-term, non-cancellable lease that transfers substantially all risks and rewards of ownership to the lessee.

| Basis | Operating Lease | Financial (Capital) Lease |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Short-term; less than the economic life of the asset | Long-term; covers most of the economic life |
| Cancellability | Cancellable at the option of the lessee (with notice) | Non-cancellable |
| Amortization | Not fully amortized; lessor recovers cost through several leases/resale | Fully amortized; lessor recovers full cost plus return from one lessee |
| Maintenance & insurance | Borne by the lessor (service lease) | Borne by the lessee (net lease) |
| Risk of obsolescence | Borne by the lessor | Borne by the lessee |
| Nature | Rental/service arrangement | Substitute for borrowing to buy the asset (debt-like) |
| Assets involved | Computers, vehicles, office equipment, assets subject to obsolescence | Land, buildings, heavy machinery, aircraft, ships |
| Ownership option | Usually no purchase option | Often has a bargain purchase or renewal option |
| Rental amount | Higher per period relative to cost (covers risk & service) | Lower; based on cost of asset + interest |
| Accounting (lessee) | Traditionally shown as rent expense (off-balance sheet) | Capitalized as asset and lease liability on balance sheet |

### Q16. The Mega Company has warrants outstanding that expire in three years. Each warrant entitles the holder to purchase two shares of common stock at an exercise price of Rs 150 per share. Determine theoretical value and premium over the theoretical value if the respective prices of common stock and warrants are:
a. Rs 140 per share and Rs 10 per warrant.
b. Rs 160 per share and Rs 40 per warrant.

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Shares per warrant (N) = 2
- Exercise price (E) = Rs 150 per share
- (a) Ps = Rs 140, warrant price = Rs 10
- (b) Ps = Rs 160, warrant price = Rs 40

**Formula:**
- Theoretical value of warrant (TV) = max[(Ps − E) × N, 0]
- Premium over theoretical value = Market price of warrant − TV
- Premium per share = Premium ÷ N; % premium = Premium ÷ TV × 100

**Step-by-step calculation:**

**(a) Ps = Rs 140, warrant = Rs 10**
1. (Ps − E) × N = (140 − 150) × 2 = −10 × 2 = −20 → negative, so TV = **Rs 0** (a warrant cannot have negative value; holder simply won't exercise).
2. Premium = 10 − 0 = **Rs 10** per warrant.
3. Premium per share = 10 ÷ 2 = Rs 5.
4. % premium over TV is not defined (TV = 0); the entire market price is premium (time value/speculative value).

**(b) Ps = Rs 160, warrant = Rs 40**
1. TV = (160 − 150) × 2 = 10 × 2 = **Rs 20**
2. Premium = 40 − 20 = **Rs 20** per warrant
3. Premium per share = 20 ÷ 2 = Rs 10
4. % premium over TV = 20 ÷ 20 × 100 = 100%

| Case | Ps (Rs) | TV (Rs) | Market price of warrant (Rs) | Premium (Rs) | Premium per share (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| a | 140 | 0 | 10 | 10 | 5 |
| b | 160 | 20 | 40 | 20 | 10 |

**Answer / Decision:** (a) TV = Rs 0, premium = Rs 10; (b) TV = Rs 20, premium = Rs 20. The warrant sells above its theoretical value because of its leverage and the chance that the share price will rise before expiry (3 years remaining).

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** TV = (Ps − E) × N, never below zero. Trap: forgetting to multiply by the number of shares per warrant (N = 2) or writing a negative TV. Shortcut: Premium = Warrant price − TV; if Ps < E, the whole warrant price is premium.

### Q17. Akash Food Company (AFC) projects an increase in sales from Rs 1,200,000 to Rs 1,800,000 but it needs an additional Rs 200,000 of current assets to support this expansion. The money can be obtained from the bank at an interest rate of 12 percent, discount interest; no compensating balance is required. Alternatively, AFC can finance the expansion by no longer taking discounts, thus increasing accounts payable. AFC purchases on the terms of 3/10, net 40, but it can delay payment for an additional 20 days and thus becoming 20 days past due without a penalty.
Based strictly on effective annual interest rate comparisons, how should AFC finance its expansion?

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Funds needed = Rs 200,000
- Bank loan: 12% discount interest, no compensating balance
- Trade credit: 3/10, net 40; payment can be stretched 20 days → paid on day 60
- Year = 360 days

**Formula:**
- Discount loan: Effective rate = Interest rate ÷ (1 − Interest rate)
- Trade credit: Cost = (d ÷ (100 − d)) × (360 ÷ (Payment day − Discount period))

**Step-by-step calculation:**

**1. Bank loan (discount interest)**
- Effective rate = 0.12 ÷ (1 − 0.12) = 0.12 ÷ 0.88 = 0.13636 = **13.64%**
- (Check: to get Rs 200,000 usable, borrow 200,000 ÷ 0.88 = Rs 227,273; interest = 227,273 × 12% = Rs 27,273; 27,273 ÷ 200,000 = 13.64%.)

**2. Trade credit (forgo discount, pay on day 60)**
- Payment day = 40 + 20 = 60
- Cost = (3 ÷ 97) × (360 ÷ (60 − 10)) = 0.030928 × 7.2 = 0.2227 = **22.27%**
- Without stretching (paid on day 40): (3 ÷ 97) × (360 ÷ 30) = 0.030928 × 12 = 37.11%

| Source | Effective annual cost |
|---|---|
| Bank loan (12% discount interest) | 13.64% |
| Trade credit, paid on day 60 (stretched) | 22.27% |
| Trade credit, paid on day 40 (not stretched) | 37.11% |

**Answer / Decision:** The bank loan costs **13.64%**, which is lower than the 22.27% cost of trade credit even after stretching. AFC should **finance the expansion with the bank loan** and continue taking the cash discounts.

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** Discount loan rate = r/(1 − r); trade credit cost = (d/(100−d)) × 360/(pay day − discount days). Trap: using the net period (40) instead of the stretched day (60), or forgetting that discount interest raises the effective rate above 12%. Shortcut: choose the source with the lower effective rate — here 13.64% < 22.27%, so borrow.

## Group C — Analytical Answer Questions (Any three, 3 × 10 = 30)

### Q18. Explain the methods of issuing securities. Also discuss the process of initial public offering.

**Solution:**

**A. Methods of issuing securities**

Firms raise long-term funds from the primary market through the following methods:

1. **Public issue (general cash offer):** Securities are offered to the general public through a prospectus.
   - **Initial Public Offering (IPO):** the first issue of shares to the public by a company, after which it becomes listed.
   - **Further Public Offering (FPO) / seasoned offering:** an additional issue by an already-listed company.

2. **Rights issue (privileged subscription):** New shares are offered first to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, usually at a price below market, honouring the preemptive right. It is cheap and preserves control. In Nepal, banks and other listed companies widely use rights issues (e.g. 1:1, 10:3).

3. **Private placement:** Securities are sold directly to a small number of institutional investors (banks, insurance companies, pension funds, mutual funds) without a public offer. It is quick, flexible and cheaper, but the issue size is limited and investors may demand higher return.

4. **Underwritten (firm-commitment / bought deal) offering:** The underwriter (investment bank/merchant banker) buys the whole issue at a fixed price and resells it to the public, bearing the risk of unsold shares. In a **bought deal**, a single underwriter buys the entire issue quickly.

5. **Best-efforts offering:** The investment banker acts only as an agent and sells as much as possible; unsold securities are returned to the issuer. Risk remains with the issuer.

6. **Competitive bidding vs. negotiated offering:** In competitive bidding, the issuer invites bids from several underwriters and awards to the highest bidder; in a negotiated offer, the terms are negotiated with one underwriter.

7. **Book building:** The price is discovered through bids from investors within a price band; the final price is fixed based on demand. SEBON introduced book building (for qualified institutional investors) in Nepal.

8. **Shelf registration:** A firm registers a large issue once and sells portions over a period (up to two years) as needed.

9. **Other methods:** Employee share issue, preferential allotment, and offers for sale by existing promoters.

**B. Process of Initial Public Offering (Nepalese context)**

1. **Decision to go public:** Board and general meeting approve the IPO, amend the memorandum/articles and convert to a public limited company if needed.
2. **Appointing an issue manager:** A SEBON-licensed merchant banker (issue manager) is appointed to manage the issue; **underwriters** are appointed to guarantee subscription.
3. **Due diligence and preparation:** The issue manager examines financial statements, legal documents, projections and business plans.
4. **Credit rating:** IPO must be graded by a credit rating agency (e.g. ICRA Nepal, CARE Ratings Nepal).
5. **Preparing the prospectus and SEBON approval:** The draft prospectus with company details, objectives, risk factors and financials is submitted to the **Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON)**, which reviews and approves it.
6. **Pricing the issue:** Fixed-price at par (Rs 100) for most companies, or at a premium (approved by SEBON) / via book building for eligible firms.
7. **Marketing and announcement:** Publication of the issue opening notice; road shows/promotion; collection centres (ASBA – member banks) and online application through **MeroShare** (CDS & Clearing Ltd.).
8. **Subscription period:** Investors apply through ASBA within the open period; quotas for employees, mutual funds, project-affected locals and Nepali workers abroad are reserved.
9. **Allotment:** If oversubscribed, shares are allotted by lottery/pro-rata under SEBON rules; refunds are released.
10. **Dematerialisation and listing:** Allotted shares are credited to investors' **demat accounts at CDS and Clearing Ltd.**, and the shares are **listed on NEPSE** for trading in the secondary market.
11. **Post-issue compliance:** Reporting to SEBON/NEPSE and continuing disclosure obligations.

**Conclusion:** Choosing the proper method depends on the issue size, cost, urgency, control considerations and market conditions; an IPO converts a private firm into a listed public company, giving it access to a wider capital base and liquidity for its shares.

### Q19. The Nepal Energy Company has announced a rights offer to raise Rs 600 million for a new Gas Station. The stock currently sells for Rs 600 per share, and there are 4.8 million shares outstanding. Par value is Rs 100.
a. What is the maximum possible subscription price? What is the minimum?
b. If the subscription price is set at Rs 500 per share, how many shares must be sold?
c. How many rights will it take to buy one share?
d. What is the value of a right?
e. What is the ex-rights price?
f. Show how a shareholder with 1,000 shares before the offering and no desire (or money) to buy additional shares is not harmed by the rights offer.

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Funds to be raised = Rs 600,000,000
- Current market price (Po, rights-on) = Rs 600
- Shares outstanding (No) = 4,800,000
- Par value = Rs 100; Subscription price (S) = Rs 500

**Formula:**
- New shares = Funds required ÷ Subscription price
- Rights per new share (N) = Old shares ÷ New shares
- Value of right (rights-on) R = (Po − S) ÷ (N + 1)
- Ex-rights price Px = (N × Po + S) ÷ (N + 1); value of right (ex-rights) R = (Px − S) ÷ N

**Step-by-step calculation:**

**(a) Maximum and minimum subscription price**
- **Maximum = Rs 600** (the current market price). If the subscription price were above Rs 600, nobody would subscribe, since shares can be bought more cheaply in the market. (In practice it is set slightly below market to ensure success.)
- **Minimum = Rs 100** (par value). Under the Companies Act, shares cannot be issued at a discount below par value. Any price between Rs 100 and Rs 600 is possible.

**(b) Number of new shares**
- New shares = 600,000,000 ÷ 500 = **1,200,000 shares**

**(c) Rights needed to buy one share**
- N = 4,800,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = **4 rights per new share** (ratio 1 new share for every 4 held)

**(d) Value of a right**
- Rights-on: R = (600 − 500) ÷ (4 + 1) = 100 ÷ 5 = **Rs 20**
- Check (ex-rights): R = (580 − 500) ÷ 4 = 80 ÷ 4 = Rs 20

**(e) Ex-rights price**
- Px = (4 × 600 + 500) ÷ (4 + 1) = (2,400 + 500) ÷ 5 = 2,900 ÷ 5 = **Rs 580**
- Or Px = Po − R = 600 − 20 = Rs 580
- (Check by total value: (4,800,000 × 600 + 600,000,000) ÷ 6,000,000 = 3,480,000,000 ÷ 6,000,000 = Rs 580)

**(f) Position of a shareholder with 1,000 shares**

| Position | Option 1: Sell rights | Option 2: Exercise rights | Option 3: Do nothing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wealth before offer | 1,000 × 600 = 600,000 | 1,000 × 600 = 600,000 | 600,000 |
| Shares after offer | 1,000 | 1,000 + 1,000/4 = 1,250 | 1,000 |
| Value of shares after | 1,000 × 580 = 580,000 | 1,250 × 580 = 725,000 | 580,000 |
| Cash from sale of rights | 1,000 × 20 = 20,000 | — | 0 |
| Cash paid for new shares | — | 250 × 500 = (125,000) | — |
| Net wealth after | **600,000** | 725,000 − 125,000 = **600,000** | **580,000** (loss 20,000) |

**Answer / Decision:**
(a) Max Rs 600, Min Rs 100; (b) 1,200,000 shares; (c) 4 rights; (d) Rs 20; (e) Rs 580; (f) A shareholder who does not want to buy simply **sells the 1,000 rights for Rs 20,000**; share value falls to Rs 580,000 but total wealth remains **Rs 600,000** — no loss. Only a shareholder who neither exercises nor sells the rights (lets them lapse) loses Rs 20,000.

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** R = (Po − S)/(N + 1) rights-on; Px = (N·Po + S)/(N + 1); R = (Px − S)/N ex-rights. Trap: using N instead of N + 1 with the rights-on price, or forgetting that the minimum price is par value. Shortcut: N = old shares ÷ new shares; Px = Po − R.

### Q20. The Kathmandu Electronic Company (KEC) intends to borrow Rs 800,000 to support its short-term financing requirements during the next year. The financing alternatives offered by the bank include:
Alternative 1: A discount interest loan with a simple interest of 15 percent and no compensating balance requirement.
Alternative 2: A 14 percent simple interest loan that has a 15 percent compensating balance requirement.
Alternative 3: Rs 1 million revolving line of credit with simple interest of 12 percent paid on the amount borrowed and a 1 percent commitment fee.
a. Compute the effective cost (rate) of each financing alternative assuming KEC borrows Rs 800,000. Which alternative should it use?
b. Discuss the factors other than cost to be considered while choosing a bank for short-term loan.

**Solution:**

**(a) Effective cost of each alternative**

**Given:**
- Usable funds needed = Rs 800,000; period = 1 year
- Alt 1: 15% discount interest
- Alt 2: 14% simple interest, 15% compensating balance (CB)
- Alt 3: Rs 1,000,000 revolving credit, 12% on amount used, 1% commitment fee on unused portion

**Formula:**
- Discount loan: Effective rate = r ÷ (1 − r)
- Simple interest with CB: Effective rate = r ÷ (1 − CB)
- Revolving credit: Effective rate = (Interest + Commitment fee) ÷ Usable funds
- Amount to borrow for required usable funds = Usable funds ÷ (1 − r or CB)

**Step-by-step calculation:**

**Alternative 1 – Discount interest loan (15%)**
- Effective rate = 0.15 ÷ (1 − 0.15) = 0.15 ÷ 0.85 = **17.65%**
- To have Rs 800,000 usable: borrow = 800,000 ÷ 0.85 = Rs 941,176
- Interest = 941,176 × 15% = Rs 141,176
- Check: 141,176 ÷ 800,000 = 17.65%
- (If face value borrowed is only Rs 800,000: interest 120,000; usable 680,000; 120,000 ÷ 680,000 = 17.65% — same rate.)

**Alternative 2 – 14% loan with 15% compensating balance**
- Effective rate = 0.14 ÷ (1 − 0.15) = 0.14 ÷ 0.85 = **16.47%**
- To have Rs 800,000 usable: borrow = 800,000 ÷ 0.85 = Rs 941,176 (CB = 141,176)
- Interest = 941,176 × 14% = Rs 131,765
- Check: 131,765 ÷ 800,000 = 16.47%

**Alternative 3 – Revolving credit (Rs 1,000,000 line)**
- Interest = 12% × 800,000 = Rs 96,000
- Unused portion = 1,000,000 − 800,000 = Rs 200,000
- Commitment fee = 1% × 200,000 = Rs 2,000
- Total cost = 96,000 + 2,000 = Rs 98,000
- Effective rate = 98,000 ÷ 800,000 = **12.25%**

| Alternative | Amount borrowed (Rs) | Annual cost (Rs) | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. 15% discount loan | 941,176 | 141,176 | 17.65% |
| 2. 14% loan, 15% CB | 941,176 | 131,765 | 16.47% |
| 3. Revolving credit 12% + 1% fee | 800,000 | 98,000 | **12.25%** |

**Answer / Decision:** Alternative 3 (revolving line of credit) has the lowest effective cost of **12.25%**, so KEC should choose **Alternative 3**.

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** Discount loan = r/(1−r); compensating balance = r/(1−CB); revolving credit = (r × used + fee% × unused)/used. Trap: charging the commitment fee on the whole Rs 1 million instead of only the unused Rs 200,000, or dividing by the gross loan instead of usable funds. Shortcut: 0.15/0.85 = 17.65%, 0.14/0.85 = 16.47%, (96,000 + 2,000)/800,000 = 12.25%.

**(b) Factors other than cost in choosing a bank**

1. **Willingness to assume risk:** Banks differ in their attitude towards risk; some are conservative while others lend to riskier borrowers.
2. **Advice and counsel:** A bank that provides financial advice and analytical services is valuable, especially to small firms.
3. **Loyalty to customers:** Whether the bank will support the borrower in bad times rather than calling the loan or refusing renewal.
4. **Specialization:** Banks specialise in particular industries (agriculture, hydropower, manufacturing, trade); a specialised bank understands the business better.
5. **Maximum loan size:** The bank's capital base and single-obligor limit determine how much it can lend to one customer; a bank big enough to meet growing needs should be chosen.
6. **Merchant banking and other services:** Availability of trade finance (LC, guarantees), cash management, foreign exchange, online banking, and capital market services.
7. **Stability and soundness of the bank:** A financially strong bank (adequate capital, low NPA) is more dependable.
8. **Relationship and convenience:** Long-term relationship, branch network, speed of decision and processing.
9. **Collateral and covenant requirements:** Less restrictive security and covenant terms give more flexibility.

### Q21. Himalaya Company can lease equipment for three years, making annual payments of Rs 150,000 per year at the end of each year or they can buy the equipment for Rs 300,000. At the end of third years, the equipment will have Rs 60,000 salvage value. The firm's before tax cost of debt is 10 percent. The company uses straight-line depreciation and has a 40 percent tax rate.
a. Calculate cost of leasing.
b. Calculate cost of purchasing. Should the machine be leased or purchased?
c. The appropriate discount rate for cash flows used in the analysis is the firm's after-tax cost of debt. Why?

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Lease payment (L) = Rs 150,000 at end of each year, n = 3 years
- Purchase cost = Rs 300,000; Salvage value (SV) = Rs 60,000 at end of year 3
- Before-tax cost of debt (kd) = 10%; Tax rate (T) = 40%
- Straight-line depreciation

**Formula:**
- Discount rate = kd(1 − T)
- PV of leasing = L(1 − T) × PVIFA(kd(1−T), n)
- Depreciation = (Cost − Salvage) ÷ n
- PV cost of purchasing = Cost − PV of depreciation tax shield − PV of salvage
- Net Advantage of Leasing (NAL) = PV cost of purchasing − PV cost of leasing

**Step-by-step calculation:**

**Discount rate:** kd(1 − T) = 10% × (1 − 0.40) = **6%**

PV factors at 6%: PVIF(6%,1) = 0.9434; PVIF(6%,2) = 0.8900; PVIF(6%,3) = 0.8396; PVIFA(6%,3) = 2.6730

**(a) Cost of leasing**
1. After-tax lease payment = 150,000 × (1 − 0.40) = Rs 90,000 per year
2. PV = 90,000 × PVIFA(6%,3) = 90,000 × 2.6730 = **Rs 240,570**

| Year | Lease payment | Tax saving (40%) | After-tax payment | PVIF @6% | PV (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 150,000 | 60,000 | 90,000 | 0.9434 | 84,906 |
| 2 | 150,000 | 60,000 | 90,000 | 0.8900 | 80,100 |
| 3 | 150,000 | 60,000 | 90,000 | 0.8396 | 75,564 |
| | | | | **Total** | **240,570** |

**(b) Cost of purchasing**

*Assumption:* Straight-line depreciation is charged on the depreciable base net of salvage, so book value at end of year 3 = salvage value and there is no tax on sale.

1. Annual depreciation = (300,000 − 60,000) ÷ 3 = 240,000 ÷ 3 = Rs 80,000
2. Depreciation tax shield = 80,000 × 0.40 = Rs 32,000 per year
3. PV of tax shield = 32,000 × 2.6730 = Rs 85,536
4. PV of salvage = 60,000 × 0.8396 = Rs 50,376
5. PV cost of purchasing = 300,000 − 85,536 − 50,376 = **Rs 164,088**

| Year | Cash outflow / (inflow) | Amount (Rs) | PVIF @6% | PV (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Purchase cost | 300,000 | 1.0000 | 300,000 |
| 1 | Depreciation tax shield | (32,000) | 0.9434 | (30,189) |
| 2 | Depreciation tax shield | (32,000) | 0.8900 | (28,480) |
| 3 | Depreciation tax shield | (32,000) | 0.8396 | (26,867) |
| 3 | Salvage value | (60,000) | 0.8396 | (50,376) |
| | **Net PV cost of purchasing** | | | **164,088** |

(Rounding year-wise PVs gives 164,088.)

**Net Advantage of Leasing (NAL)** = PV cost of purchasing − PV cost of leasing = 164,088 − 240,570 = **− Rs 76,482**

*Note (alternative assumption):* If depreciation is charged on the full Rs 300,000 (Rs 100,000 per year), tax shield = 40,000 × 2.6730 = 106,920; the salvage Rs 60,000 is then fully taxable, after-tax salvage = 36,000, PV = 36,000 × 0.8396 = 30,226; cost of purchasing = 300,000 − 106,920 − 30,226 = Rs 162,854. The decision (buy) does not change.

**Answer / Decision:** PV cost of leasing = **Rs 240,570**; PV cost of purchasing = **Rs 164,088**. Since purchasing is cheaper by Rs 76,482 (NAL is negative), Himalaya Company should **purchase (borrow and buy) the equipment**, not lease it.

**(c) Why the after-tax cost of debt is the discount rate**
1. **Lease is a substitute for debt:** A financial lease is a contractual, fixed obligation just like debt-service payments; failure to pay leads to loss of the asset or default. Leasing displaces the firm's debt capacity, so it should be compared with borrowing to buy.
2. **Low risk of the cash flows:** Lease payments, depreciation tax shields and the purchase price are known with (near) certainty, so they have about the same risk as the firm's debt — not the risk of the firm's operating cash flows. Hence WACC (which includes equity risk) is not appropriate.
3. **After-tax flows require an after-tax rate:** All cash flows in the analysis are after-tax (lease payments are deductible, interest is deductible), so consistency requires discounting at kd(1 − T).
4. **Opportunity cost of financing:** The firm's alternative to leasing is a secured loan; its after-tax cost is the true opportunity cost of the funds.
(Salvage value is riskier, and some analysts discount it at a higher rate such as WACC.)

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** Discount rate = kd(1−T); PV lease = L(1−T) × PVIFA; PV buy = Cost − (Dep × T × PVIFA) − SV × PVIF; NAL = PV buy − PV lease. Trap: discounting at 10% (pre-tax) or forgetting the tax shield on lease payments and depreciation. Shortcut: choose the lower PV cost — positive NAL → lease, negative NAL → buy.

## Group D — Comprehensive / Case Study (20)

### Q22. Read the following case carefully and answer the question that follow:
The Himalayan Herbal Company (HHC) has grown rapidly during the past five years. Recently, it has discovered new investment opportunity. Company is planning to increase debt to finance the new project. There are two alternatives to raise debt capital for the company: i. convertible debenture, ii. bonds with warrants. HH Company is contemplating raising Rs 10 million by means of a debt issue. It has the following alternatives:

a. A 20-year, 8 percent convertible debenture issue with a Rs 50 conversion price and Rs 1,000 face value.
b. A 20-year, 10 percent straight bond issue. Each Rs 1,000 bond has a detachable warrant to purchase four shares for Rs 50 a share.

The company has a 50 percent tax rate, and its stock is currently selling at Rs 40 a share. Its earnings before interest and taxes are a constant 20 percent of its total capitalization, which currently appears as follows:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Common stock (par Rs 5) | Rs 5,000,000 |
| Additional paid-in capital | 10,000,000 |
| Retained earnings | 15,000,000 |
| Total | Rs 30,000,000 |

i. Show the capitalizations before and after conversion of convertible.
ii. Show the capitalizations before and after exercise of warrants.
iii. Compute earnings per share currently and before and after conversion of convertible and exercise of warrant.
iv. Describe the reasons for issuing warrants and convertible.

**Solution:**

**Given:**
- Debt issue = Rs 10,000,000 → number of Rs 1,000 bonds = 10,000,000 ÷ 1,000 = 10,000
- Shares outstanding = 5,000,000 ÷ 5 = **1,000,000 shares**
- Convertible: 8% coupon, conversion price Rs 50
- Bonds with warrants: 10% coupon, 4 warrants (shares) per bond at Rs 50
- EBIT = 20% of total capitalization; Tax = 50%

**Formula:**
- Conversion ratio = Face value ÷ Conversion price
- New shares on conversion = Number of bonds × Conversion ratio
- New shares on exercise = Number of bonds × Shares per warrant; Cash received = New shares × Exercise price
- Increase in common stock = New shares × Par; Increase in APIC = New shares × (Issue price − Par)
- EPS = (EBIT − Interest)(1 − T) ÷ Number of shares

#### (i) Capitalization — convertible debentures

**Step-by-step calculation:**
1. Conversion ratio = 1,000 ÷ 50 = 20 shares per debenture
2. New shares = 10,000 × 20 = 200,000 shares
3. Increase in common stock = 200,000 × Rs 5 = Rs 1,000,000
4. Increase in APIC = 200,000 × (50 − 5) = 200,000 × 45 = Rs 9,000,000 (total Rs 10,000,000 = debt converted)

| Item | Before conversion (Rs) | After conversion (Rs) |
|---|---|---|
| Convertible debentures (8%) | 10,000,000 | — |
| Common stock (par Rs 5) | 5,000,000 | 6,000,000 |
| Additional paid-in capital | 10,000,000 | 19,000,000 |
| Retained earnings | 15,000,000 | 15,000,000 |
| **Total capitalization** | **40,000,000** | **40,000,000** |
| Shares outstanding | 1,000,000 | 1,200,000 |

Conversion merely transfers Rs 10 million from debt to equity; total capitalization is unchanged.

#### (ii) Capitalization — bonds with warrants

**Step-by-step calculation:**
1. New shares on exercise = 10,000 bonds × 4 = 40,000 shares
2. Cash received = 40,000 × Rs 50 = Rs 2,000,000
3. Increase in common stock = 40,000 × 5 = Rs 200,000
4. Increase in APIC = 40,000 × (50 − 5) = 40,000 × 45 = Rs 1,800,000

| Item | Before exercise (Rs) | After exercise (Rs) |
|---|---|---|
| Bonds (10%) | 10,000,000 | 10,000,000 |
| Common stock (par Rs 5) | 5,000,000 | 5,200,000 |
| Additional paid-in capital | 10,000,000 | 11,800,000 |
| Retained earnings | 15,000,000 | 15,000,000 |
| **Total capitalization** | **40,000,000** | **42,000,000** |
| Shares outstanding | 1,000,000 | 1,040,000 |

Warrants are detachable, so the bonds remain outstanding after exercise and new cash of Rs 2 million increases total capitalization.

#### (iii) Earnings per share

**Step-by-step calculation:**
- Currently: EBIT = 20% × 30,000,000 = Rs 6,000,000
- Convertible (before and after): EBIT = 20% × 40,000,000 = Rs 8,000,000; interest before conversion = 8% × 10,000,000 = Rs 800,000
- Warrants before exercise: EBIT = Rs 8,000,000; interest = 10% × 10,000,000 = Rs 1,000,000
- Warrants after exercise: EBIT = 20% × 42,000,000 = Rs 8,400,000; interest = Rs 1,000,000

| Particulars | Currently | Convertible: before conversion | Convertible: after conversion | Warrants: before exercise | Warrants: after exercise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total capitalization | 30,000,000 | 40,000,000 | 40,000,000 | 40,000,000 | 42,000,000 |
| EBIT (20%) | 6,000,000 | 8,000,000 | 8,000,000 | 8,000,000 | 8,400,000 |
| Less: Interest | 0 | 800,000 | 0 | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
| EBT | 6,000,000 | 7,200,000 | 8,000,000 | 7,000,000 | 7,400,000 |
| Less: Tax (50%) | 3,000,000 | 3,600,000 | 4,000,000 | 3,500,000 | 3,700,000 |
| Net income | 3,000,000 | 3,600,000 | 4,000,000 | 3,500,000 | 3,700,000 |
| Shares outstanding | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 | 1,200,000 | 1,000,000 | 1,040,000 |
| **EPS (Rs)** | **3.00** | **3.60** | **3.33** | **3.50** | **3.56** |

**Comment:**
- Both issues raise EPS above the current Rs 3.00 because the new Rs 10 million earns 20% before tax while costing only 8% or 10%.
- **Convertible:** EPS is highest before conversion (Rs 3.60, lowest coupon); on conversion EPS falls to Rs 3.33 because 200,000 new shares are issued (dilution) although interest is saved.
- **Warrants:** EPS rises from Rs 3.50 to Rs 3.56 on exercise because exercise brings in Rs 2 million of new cash (which earns 20%) while the bonds remain, and only 40,000 shares are added.
- Fully diluted, the warrant alternative (Rs 3.56) gives a higher EPS than the convertible (Rs 3.33).

> 💡 **Exam Hint (remember):** Conversion ratio = Face value ÷ Conversion price; CS ↑ = new shares × par, APIC ↑ = new shares × (price − par); EPS = (EBIT − I)(1 − T) ÷ shares. Trap: on conversion, interest disappears and total capitalization stays the same; on warrant exercise, the bond and its interest remain and capitalization increases by the cash received. Shortcut: Convertible → debt moves to equity (no new cash); Warrant → new cash comes in (debt stays).

#### (iv) Reasons for issuing warrants and convertibles

**A. Reasons for issuing convertibles**
1. **Sweetener / lower interest cost:** The conversion option lets the firm issue debt at a lower coupon (8% versus 10% for straight bonds) and with fewer restrictive covenants.
2. **Deferred equity financing ("equity through the back door"):** The firm effectively sells common stock at a future date, at a conversion price (Rs 50) higher than the current market price (Rs 40), so fewer shares are issued than in a direct equity issue now — less dilution.
3. **Delayed dilution of EPS and control:** Dilution occurs only when the project starts generating earnings and conversion takes place.
4. **Forcing conversion with the call feature:** Once the share price exceeds the conversion price, the firm can call the bonds to force conversion, eliminating debt and improving the capital structure.
5. **Suitable for growing firms and low-rated firms:** Rapidly growing companies like HHC with uncertain risk can raise funds on acceptable terms; investors get downside protection of a bond and upside of equity.
6. **Reduced agency conflicts:** Convertibles reduce the bondholder–shareholder conflict over risky investments.

**B. Reasons for issuing warrants**
1. **Sweetener to debt:** Warrants make bonds more attractive, so the firm can issue them at lower interest, with fewer covenants, or when it otherwise could not borrow (small or risky firms).
2. **Additional cash inflow:** On exercise, the firm receives fresh capital (Rs 2 million for HHC) while the debt remains outstanding — useful for a growing firm that needs more funds as it grows.
3. **Sale of stock at a higher price:** Exercise price (Rs 50) is set above the current price (Rs 40), so equity is raised at a premium.
4. **Delayed dilution:** Warrants are exercised only when the share price rises above the exercise price — usually when the firm is performing well.
5. **Detachability and marketability:** Detachable warrants can be traded separately, increasing the attractiveness of the issue.
6. **Used in mergers, reorganizations and venture financing** as compensation or inducement to underwriters, promoters and investors.

**Conclusion for HHC:** Both instruments let HHC borrow cheaply now and raise equity later at a premium price. The convertible has a lower interest cost and eliminates the debt on conversion (lower leverage), while the warrant alternative brings in additional cash and gives higher fully diluted EPS (Rs 3.56 vs Rs 3.33) but keeps the debt and higher interest burden. The choice depends on whether HHC wants to reduce leverage (convertible) or needs more total funds and higher EPS (warrants).
